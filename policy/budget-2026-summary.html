Context

The Chancellor of the Exchequer, John Healey, presented Budget 2026 to the House of Commons on 28 October 2026, his first Budget. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) published its Economic and Fiscal Outlook the same day, and the government published the Fiscal Devolution Roadmap alongside the Budget.1

This summary sets out the economic background, the main announcements with their costings, the OBR's forecasts and the reaction to the Budget, and closes with what it means for Gateshead residents. Full Budget documents are available at gov.uk.

1. Economic Context Ahead of the Budget

The UK economy grew by 0.5% in the second quarter of 2026, after 0.6% in the first quarter.2 Output in the second quarter was 1.4% higher than a year earlier.2 The government described this as the joint fastest growth in the G7 across the first half of the year.3

Inflation, measured by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI), had been falling and reached 2.6% in June 2026, before rising to 3.5% in September as the conflict in the Middle East pushed up oil and gas prices.4 The Budget document says the majority of that rise came from motor fuel, and that consumer prices are now around 33% higher than their 2020 average, with household energy 73% higher.5

Wholesale oil prices rose by around 60% and UK gas prices by nearly 200% between the disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in February and October.6 Interest rate expectations and government borrowing costs rose with them.6

2. Budget Statement and Summary of Announcements

The Chancellor opened his statement by setting out three aims.

‘It is a Budget built on fiscal discipline, which matches immediate action today with clear direction for the future: first, by giving Britain some breathing space on the cost of living and the cost of business; secondly, by backing the enterprise of British business to unleash innovation and growth in every postcode; and thirdly, by tackling the big challenges on energy, national security, public service procurement and transformation, and housing.’7

The Budget document says the Budget ‘can reject broad-based tax rises and protect most individuals and businesses from tax increases’, with no rise in the headline rates of income tax, National Insurance contributions or VAT.8 It says all policy at the Budget is fully funded over the forecast.9

Table 6.1 of the Budget lists 98 costed policy decisions. Taken together they increase borrowing by £7,595 million in 2026-27 and reduce it by £7,350 million in 2031-32.10 The OBR describes the package as broadly neutral over the forecast as a whole, with an average net impact of 0.02% of GDP.11

2.1 Main Revenue-Raising Measures

Costings are the Exchequer effect in each year, as published in Table 6.1.10

  • Tax Compliance – A package of HMRC measures to close the gap between tax due and tax paid, the largest being more frequent self-assessment payments from April 2029. It raises £100 million in 2027-28, rising to £3,160 million in 2031-3212
  • Writing-Down Allowances – The main rate of writing-down allowances falls by two percentage points to 12% from April 2027, while the first-year allowance rises from 40% to 50%. This raises £765 million in 2027-28 and £1,120 million in 2031-3213
  • Bank Surcharge – The bank corporation tax surcharge rises from 3% to 6% for 15 months from 1 January 2027, raising £305 million in 2026-27 and £950 million in 2027-28. The government says it ‘will not be extended’14
  • Savings and Property Income – Income tax rates on savings and property income rise by a further two percentage points from April 2027, to 24%, 44% and 49%. Together they raise approximately £1,130 million in 2028-29 and £1,045 million in 2031-32 (our sums). The government says 84% of income taxpayers are unaffected and around half the revenue comes from the top 10% of households15
  • Company Distributions – Distributions from companies to individual shareholders are taxed on a consistent basis from April 2028, raising £800 million in 2029-3016
  • Foreign Branches and Sovereign Investors – UK companies can no longer set losses of overseas branches against UK profits from January 2027 (£480 million in 2027-28), and certain UK property income and gains of foreign governments come into tax from January 2029 (£690 million in 2029-30)17
  • Online Marketplaces and Private Hire – Online marketplaces collect VAT on UK sellers with over £90,000 of sales from October 2027 (£320 million in 2028-29), and larger private hire operators pay VAT on the full fare from January 2028 (£385 million in 2028-29)18
  • Machine Games Duty – The rate rises from 20% to 25% on higher-stake machines from April 2027, raising £135 million in 2027-2819

Among other measures, the cash ISA limit stays at £20,000 and the lifetime limit for Business Asset Disposal Relief falls from £1 million to £500,000 from April 2027.20

2.2 Spending Decisions and Other Measures

  • Household Energy Payment – A £100 payment for households in council tax bands A to D in England, delivered by councils through the council tax system from January 2027, with £100 million for councils to give further targeted support. It costs £2,625 million in 2026-27. Up to 20 million households, around 80% of households in England, are eligible21
  • VAT on Electricity and Fuel Duty – The 0% rate of VAT on domestic electricity is extended to 1 July 2027, which the government expects to take around £50 off the April 2027 price cap (£755 million in 2026-27). The temporary 5p cut in fuel duty is extended to 1 July 2027 and the planned RPI increase deferred (£780 million in 2027-28)22
  • Gas Standing Charges – The government has asked Ofgem to abolish gas standing charges on household bills, which average £108 a year, aiming for the end of 2027, with the costs moved to unit rates23
  • Bus Fares – Single bus fares in England outside London are capped at £2 for 2027, funded from savings in the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero24
  • Business Rates for High Streets – From April 2027, Small Business Rates Relief thresholds rise to £18,000 for full relief and £21,000 for tapered relief for retail, hospitality and leisure properties in England, bills for those properties are frozen in real terms for two years, and relief for pubs, social clubs and live music venues rises to 35%. The government says around 70,000 more high street businesses will pay no business rates. The package costs approximately £485 million in 2027-28 and £665 million in 2028-29 (our sums)25
  • National Living Wage – The National Living Wage rises by 3.9% to £13.21 an hour from 1 April 2027, worth £910 a year to a full-time worker, with around 2.3 million workers expected to benefit26
  • Industrial Electricity – The British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme cuts electricity bills by up to 25% for over 10,000 energy-intensive manufacturers from April 2027, costing £1,235 million in 2027-28 including a backdated payment27
  • Defence – The remaining £4.7 billion of the £15 billion Defence Investment Plan is funded by a further 1% cut to departmental capital budgets from 2027-28 to 2029-30 and £1.7 billion of Treasury capital reserve28
  • Administration Budgets – Departmental administration budgets fall by 20% in real terms by 2029-30, saving an additional £595 million in 2029-3029
  • Housing – An extra £1.5 billion for around 10,000 social and affordable homes in England, prioritised for Social Rent and council homes, and a £4.7 billion Your First Home equity loan scheme for around 70,000 first-time buyers, with price caps set from local authority median prices30
  • Rough Sleeping – An additional £432 million across 2027-28 and 2028-29, on top of £442 million announced since July 202631
  • Investment – The National Wealth Fund and British Business Bank are recapitalised by £20.4 billion, including £2 billion for a new Energy Wealth Fund and a £5 billion increase in the British Business Bank's capacity32

Fiscal Devolution – The Fiscal Devolution Roadmap sets out a new funding model for mayors. From April 2027, mayors will be able to receive a share of business rates income in place of three existing grants, plus a 20% share of business rates growth, subject to consultation and local agreement. The Budget document and the roadmap also describe this as a 20% share of locally retained business rates.33 From 2028-29, mayoral strategic authorities will be assigned a share of basic rate income tax from earnings and pensions in their area, with a share of the higher rate under consideration and bespoke starting allocations set at the 2027 Spending Review. The government intends local and regional government to keep 100% of business rates before the end of the decade, with councils keeping at least 70%, and the government plans to legislate for all strategic authorities to have the power to introduce an Overnight Visitor Levy. The Budget also confirms £285 million in 2027-28 for Good Growth Funds and a £1.5 billion borrowing facility for mayoral authorities from April 2028. The government will publish its Rewiring the State Delivery Plan before the end of the year.34

State Pension – The Triple Lock continues to April 2029. From April 2030 an adjusted Triple Lock uprates the State Pension by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the amount needed to keep its value relative to earnings at its April 2029 level. The government says this will help fund a National Care Service in England.35

2.3 Fiscal Rules and Headroom

The government has two fiscal rules. In the Chancellor's words, they are ‘the stability rule that day-to-day spending must be covered by tax receipts; and the investment rule that Government debt, defined as public sector net financial liabilities, must be falling as percentage of GDP’.7 Both now apply in the third year of the forecast, 2029-30.36

Headroom is the margin by which a rule is met, the room left before it would be broken. The OBR judges that the stability rule is met by £14.5 billion in 2029-30, against £21.7 billion when the rules were last assessed in November 2025. It puts the chance of meeting the rule at 56%, against 59% in November 2025. The investment rule is met by £24.4 billion, unchanged.36

The Chancellor said he had ‘chosen to use some of the headroom established last year to absorb the impact of the Iran war to protect the economy and to protect family budgets’.7 The OBR notes the margin is around half the average left by Chancellors since 2010.37

3. Economic and Fiscal Forecasts

The OBR published its forecast after the Chancellor's statement. Its overview opens with this assessment.

‘The UK’s economic and fiscal position remains very challenging. Since 2008, repeated adverse shocks have contributed to persistently weak GDP growth and elevated levels of government borrowing.’38
‘The outlook for the economy and public finances is uncertain, with significantly better or worse outcomes of broadly equal probability.’38

The OBR's central forecast has weaker growth and higher inflation in the near term than its March forecast, because of higher energy prices and interest rates. It expects the level of GDP to be broadly back on its March path by 2030. Real household disposable income per person falls slightly in the near term and in 2030-31 is about ¾% below the March forecast.39

Public sector net borrowing is forecast to fall from 4.2% of GDP (£133 billion) in 2026-27 to 1.8% (£67 billion) in 2030-31, higher than in March in every year. Debt peaks at around 97% of GDP in 2028-29. Debt interest spending is around £14 billion a year higher than in March on average. The OBR also judges that the welfare cap is on course to be missed by £3 billion in 2029-30, once the margin is included.41

On public spending, the OBR notes that on the government's assumptions, day-to-day spending outside protected areas would fall by 2.6% a year in real terms, after allowing for inflation, in the final three years of the forecast, and would need £11 billion more in 2031-32 to avoid real cuts from 2028-29.42

4. Reaction to the Budget

4.1 Political Reaction

Responding for the Opposition, Kemi Badenoch said ‘We are overspent, overborrowed, overtaxed and drowning in an out-of-control welfare system’. She welcomed the decisions on ISAs and fuel duty.43

For the Liberal Democrats, Ed Davey said ‘the biggest single thing the Government could do to fix our economy is to fix our trading relationship with Europe and take us back into the single market and a customs union’.43

For the SNP, Kirsty Blackman said ‘People desperately need support with the cost of living’ and noted that real household disposable income is forecast to fall in the short term.43

For Reform UK, Richard Tice said ‘This is a Government who are completely out of control in terms of spending’, while crediting the approval of the Jackdaw gas field.43

For the DUP, Sammy Wilson said ‘the Chancellor has brought forward a Budget, albeit one that is inadequate’, adding that 60% of people in Northern Ireland heat their homes with oil and would not be helped by the energy measures. For Plaid Cymru, Llinos Medi said ‘it has delivered one of the worst funding settlements for Wales in years’.43

4.2 Other Selected Reaction

Think Tanks

The Institute for Fiscal Studies said ‘This was broadly the Budget that many were expecting’ and that it had ‘a raft of fiddly measures but very little that will move the dial on growth’. It described the planned public spending as ‘very tight’ and called next year's Spending Review ‘a crunch point’. On devolution it described ‘a balanced approach to devolving tax revenues to mayors’ that ‘represents a real change in the financial stake that local areas have in economic growth’.44

The Resolution Foundation called it ‘a mid-table Budget that is the 19th biggest of the 34 fiscal events since 2010’. It said the £100 payment was ‘badly targeted’, noting that one in five lower-income households in London and the South East live in band E and above and miss out, against one in twenty in the North East, North West and Yorkshire and The Humber. It described the higher tax rates on savings and property income as ‘sensible’.45

The Centre for Policy Studies said ‘This felt like a peacetime Budget in a wartime world’ and that the numbers ‘rest on public spending projections that are increasingly fictional’.46

Trade Unions and Business Groups

The TUC said ‘taxing banks' bumper profits to cut energy bills is plain common sense’, and warned that ‘any cuts to departmental capital budgets will be met with real concern’.47

The CBI said ‘today's Budget does not meet the scale of that ambition’ and that business rates relief for retail, hospitality and leisure firms ‘will be welcomed but misses the chance to deliver the wholesale reform to the system we desperately need’.48

The British Chambers of Commerce called it ‘a “steady as you go” budget, sending the right signals to business, but lacking big calls that would really shift the economic dial’, and said ‘most businesses won’t notice this budget at all’.49

5. Impact on Gateshead Residents

  • Household Energy Payment – 91,469 of Gateshead's 95,628 dwellings are in council tax bands A to D, so the £100 payment is worth up to approximately £9.1 million here, delivered by the council from January 2027. The Resolution Foundation finds that one in twenty lower-income households in the North East, North West and Yorkshire and The Humber live in band E and above and miss out, against one in five in London and the South East50
  • National Living Wage – In April 2026 the lowest-paid tenth of Gateshead residents in work earned £12.91 an hour or less (the 10th percentile), and the lowest-paid fifth £13.46 or less (the 20th percentile), against £12.99 and £13.89 in England. That suggests roughly 1 in 10 to 1 in 5 resident jobs, approximately 8,000 to 16,000, were paid below the new rate of £13.21 at April 2026 pay. The upper end of that range is less certain, because the survey's confidence interval for the 20th percentile, the range the true figure is likely to sit in, includes £13.2151
  • Business Rates – Retail, hospitality and leisure property, which is taxed on lower multipliers (the pence in the pound applied to a property's value), makes up 32.3% of Gateshead's rateable value, the total value on which business rates are charged, so the two-year real-terms freeze on bills for these properties reaches a large part of our business base, with the higher relief thresholds helping the smaller ones. The Budget does not say whether councils will be compensated for the income lost to the new reliefs52
  • Travel Costs – 32.4% of Gateshead households have no car or van, against 27.6% in the North East and 23.5% in England, so the £2 bus fare cap matters more here. The 67.6% of households with a car or van gain from the extended 5p fuel duty cut to July 202753
  • Council Funding and Devolution – The mayoral share of business rates passes to the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority (NEMSA), and the roadmap says it will not affect councils' core funding. The North East is in the first wave of mayoral business rates retention zones, the areas where the mayor keeps a share of business rates growth. Spending on special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) moves to central government funding from 2028-29. The government will reimburse 90% of councils' SEND deficits built up to the end of 2025-26, but has made no commitment on deficits built up after that point54
  • Other North East Items – The Budget confirms over £85 million for the Offshore Renewable Energy Catapult in Blyth, the start of A66 dualling between the M6 and A1(M) in November 2026, and the next International Investment Summit in Newcastle in spring 202755
