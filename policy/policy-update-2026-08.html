An unusual month. There was a change of Prime Minister on 20 July 2026 and a machinery-of-government reorganisation the following week. Four weeks produced more change to the framework Gateshead's economy sits inside than the previous four months: the department that owns local growth policy, the tax base councils and mayors will be funded from, the business rates bill on a whole class of high-street premises, and the survey behind our labour market statistics. Most of it is announced rather than legislated, and the detail arrives at the Budget on 28 October.

Executive summary

  • Fiscal devolution is now a dated programme, and the mechanism is still missing. Mayors retain a share of business rates from April 2027 and receive a share of income tax from 2028, "replacing grants from central government". That substitution is the whole question: it is not additional money, and for a low-tax-base authority the equalisation design decides whether it is a gain or a loss. The Government has said only that it "will consider how these growth incentives can be balanced with the need for fairness between places". The detail lands at the Budget on Wednesday 28 October.
  • Local growth policy has left MHCLG and the Treasury. No10 North opened at Heron House, Manchester on 23 July, taking local economic growth and devolution strategy out of the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government and local growth policy out of HM Treasury, and reviving the National Economic Council with mayors at the table. The Northern Growth Strategy — which carries the £120m for the Newcastle–Gateshead Mayoral Development Zone — was a Treasury and MHCLG product; its autumn update now comes from a different owner.
  • Twenty-eight days to submit on high streets. The Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee's Revitalising High Streets inquiry closes to written evidence at 11.59pm on 7 September 2026. Business rates, High Street Rental Auctions, compulsory purchase, the community right to buy, unwanted retail uses and Pride in Place are all in scope — the closest fit to the visitor and placemaking objectives of anything on the current watch. A draft evidence pack already exists.
  • A 20% business rates cut lands on premises we have no baseline for. From April 2027 pubs, social clubs and live music venues in England get a 20% discount, on top of a real-terms freeze for 2027/28 and 2028/29. It is the first policy change aimed squarely at the priority local centres, and the team cannot currently say how many qualifying hereditaments Felling, Winlaton and Chopwell contain. Part of the funding comes from reviewing reliefs for businesses judged less beneficial to communities, vape shops named among them — which is the same argument the high streets inquiry is running.
  • Two household measures with a measurable inflation effect. VAT on domestic electricity goes from 5% to zero on 1 October 2026, worth around £45 on the October price cap and around 0.10 percentage points off CPI. The £2 bus fare cap returns in January 2027 across England outside London, backed by £400m. Both feed the cost-of-living index directly; the bus cap is also a real change in the cost of reaching Gateshead's centres.
  • Two open evidence items from July are now closed, and a new one opens. The Transformed Labour Force Survey readiness assessment publishes on 11 August. Quarterly regional GDP is being reinstated, but at ITL1 — the North East as a whole, not Gateshead. Newly opened: ONS intends to rebuild the headline productivity statistic on a component-based method, timed for November, the same month our 2024 subregional productivity figure arrives.
  • Employment Rights Act commencements have started to bite. Electronic and workplace balloting for statutory trade union ballots takes effect on 25 August 2026 — narrow in itself, but the first commencement inside our reporting window on a timetable that runs through 2027.

1. Fiscal devolution: dated, substantive, and still missing the mechanism

On 30 July 2026 the Prime Minister announced that mayors will receive a share of income tax, with the direction set out the following day in the Cabinet Statement Rewiring the State (31 July 2026). The dated commitments are:

FromWhat
April 2027Mayors begin retaining a share of business rates
Spring 2027Strategic authorities gain the power to introduce an Overnight Visitor Levy; plans for how revenues will be invested due by March 2028
2028A share of local income tax for every mayor, "replacing grants from central government"
28 October 2026Budget, with the fiscal devolution roadmap and the autumn devolution white paper
Read the substitution, not the headline. The statement's own words are that this begins "by replacing grants from central government with a share of local income tax for every mayor beginning in 2028". This is not new money layered on top of the existing settlement — it exchanges a needs-assessed grant for a locally-raised revenue share. Retention-based systems reward a buoyant tax base and low service demand. Gateshead has neither, so the question of whether this helps or hurts turns entirely on the equalisation design, and the equalisation design does not yet exist.

On fairness the statement commits only to a consideration: the Government "will consider how these growth incentives can be balanced with the need for fairness between places, recognising that there will be different starting points". No formula, no baseline, no reset period, and no statement of which areas are in the first wave. On the income tax share it is not yet established whether mayors receive assigned revenue against a fixed baseline, a growth increment only, or genuine rate-setting powers — three designs with materially different consequences for a place whose tax base grows slowly. The Institute for Fiscal Studies' response and the Commons Library briefing CBP-10937 are the neutral summaries.

Spending Review, not Budget, for the income tax detail. The roadmap published at the Budget covers the overall approach to revenue assignment and rates retention and the business rates share. The detailed arrangements for retained income tax are confirmed at the Spending Review, whose date has not been announced. Two reads are therefore needed, not one.

What this means from an economic policy lens

  • Derive the tax-base position before the Budget, not after. The single most useful number the team can hold on 28 October is Gateshead's income tax base per taxpayer against the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority and UK averages, from HMRC's Survey of Personal Incomes, together with the borough's share of the NECA-area total on both a basic-rate-only and an all-income-tax basis. The team has a working estimate from the Budget watch; it needs re-deriving from source and dating before it goes anywhere. Logged as a work item.
  • The Overnight Visitor Levy is now a dated power, not a possibility. Spring 2027 for the power, March 2028 for revenue plans. Any Quays visitor evidence intended to inform a North East levy case needs a baseline captured well before then. V-O1
  • Business rates retention interacts with section 3. Cutting the rates bill on pubs, clubs and live music venues from April 2027 and giving mayors a share of rates income from April 2027 are the same reform arriving from two directions. Who bears the cost of the relief once retention starts is a question worth asking early.

2. Who owns local growth policy: it moved to Manchester

A machinery-of-government change was announced on 21 July 2026 (written statement HLWS298), and No10 North opened at Heron House in central Manchester on 23 July 2026. Local economic growth and devolution strategy move out of the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, and local growth policy out of HM Treasury, into the new office; an Office for the Prime Minister and the Cabinet is created to house No10, No10 North and the Cabinet Office teams serving the Prime Minister and Cabinet. The National Economic Council is revived, bringing senior ministers and regional mayors together.

The practical consequence for this team is one of provenance rather than policy. The Northern Growth Strategy — which commits £120m to the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority working with Newcastle and Gateshead councils, anchored on the proposed Newcastle–Gateshead Mayoral Development Zone — was a Treasury and MHCLG product, and its invitation for views closed on 31 July 2026. The promised autumn update now issues from a different owner. Whether it survives in the form consulted on is the open question, and it was already on the watch list before the move.

What this means from an economic policy lens

  • Re-check departmental attributions before citing. Any product citing MHCLG or HM Treasury as the owner of a local growth document should be re-checked at the point of publication; landing pages are being re-badged.
  • Mayors are inside the machine now. The North East Mayor at a revived National Economic Council changes the route by which Gateshead evidence can travel upward — it runs through the Mayoral Strategic Authority more than before, not less. L-O2
  • The £120m and the Mayoral Development Zone remain proposed. Boundaries are still not formally defined. The baseline-capture argument made in the July edition stands and gets more urgent, not less, with an owner change in the middle of it. V-O1 V-O3

3. Business rates and household costs: three measures with a Gateshead incidence

3.1 A 20% business rates discount for pubs, social clubs and live music venues

Announced on 23 July 2026 (gov.uk), taking effect for the 2027/28 rates year from April 2027. The Government's figures are a benefit to nearly 32,000 venues in England, a saving of around £1,100 in the first year for a typical pub, and a cost to the Exchequer of roughly £100m a year. It sits on top of the 15% relief announced in January 2026 for 2026/27 and a real-terms freeze in bills for qualifying pubs and live music venues in 2027/28 and 2028/29. The very largest live music venues are excluded, and the Government has not yet said where that line falls. Funding comes partly from a review of reliefs available to businesses it judges less beneficial to local communities, with vape shops named.

The baseline we do not hold. This is the first policy change since the visitor objectives were set that lands directly on premises in the priority local centres, and the team cannot presently say how many qualifying hereditaments Felling, Winlaton and Chopwell contain, nor what proportion of their rateable value they represent. The Valuation Office Agency rating list gives this at property level and is free. Establishing it before April 2027 turns an announcement into a measurable local effect. V-O2

3.2 VAT removed from domestic electricity, 1 October 2026

VAT on household electricity falls from 5% to zero from 1 October 2026 (gov.uk), timed for the October Ofgem price cap. The Government's estimates are around £45 off the annual cap, around 0.10 percentage points off CPI and around 0.14 off RPI, with suppliers expected to pass it through to fixed-tariff customers as well.

Duration caveat, carried from the July edition and still unresolved: the gov.uk announcement does not state an end date. Reports of a six-month cut ending in March 2027 come from tax-practitioner and press sources — do not publish an end date as fact.

3.3 The £2 bus fare cap returns, January 2027

From January 2027 a £2 single-fare cap applies on participating services across England outside London, backed by £400m, replacing the £3 cap — a reduction of a third in the maximum fare (gov.uk).

What this means from an economic policy lens

  • Cost-of-living index inputs, both dated. The VAT change is a step reduction in the electricity component from October and the bus cap a step reduction in transport from January. Both belong in the index's forward path with their announcement dates recorded, and both are policy effects rather than market movements — label them as such so the index is not read as demand easing.
  • The bus cap is an access measure as much as a cost measure. In a borough where a material share of trips to the centres are by bus, a third off the maximum single fare is a change in the effective catchment of Gateshead's centres. It is testable against footfall if a baseline exists. V-O2
  • Watch the relief review. Funding the pub relief by reviewing support to "less beneficial" businesses puts national policy behind the same proposition the high streets inquiry is testing (section 4). If the review produces a defined class of use, it becomes a planning and rates lever the Council can plan around. V-O3

4. High streets: a live inquiry that fits the objectives almost exactly — closes 7 September

Twenty-eight days. The Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee's Revitalising High Streets inquiry closes to written evidence at 11.59pm on Monday 7 September 2026. A draft evidence pack already exists in the team's working folder. The decision needed is whether it is submitted, by whom, and in whose name.

The inquiry, launched in July 2026, covers the proliferation of unwanted retail and service uses — betting shops, vape shops and so-called fake barbers are named — and their effect on high streets and community pride; approaches to regeneration including design codes, street furniture and pedestrianisation; the effectiveness of local authority powers, specifically High Street Rental Auctions, compulsory purchase orders and the community right to buy; the impact of business rates changes on high street businesses; and the Pride in Place programme.

Every one of those touches something the team already holds evidence on. The community right to buy and the ban on upward-only rent reviews arrived in the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026, covered in the July edition. The business rates change is section 3.1 above. Pride in Place is live in Gateshead through the Wrekenton and Beacon Lough neighbourhood board, which confirmed its membership and boundaries on 17 July 2026.

What this means from an economic policy lens

  • This is the best-fitting external opportunity on the current watch. It maps onto the visitor objectives more precisely than any funding call — measurable footfall, occupancy and business performance in priority local centres is exactly what the Committee is asking about. V-O2 V-O3
  • Submitting evidence is a different act from publishing analysis. Written evidence to a select committee is a formal submission on the Council's behalf; the decision on whether it goes in the Council's name sits above this team.
  • The honest gap is the same one section 3.1 names. Any submission arguing the priority-centre case is stronger with a hereditament and vacancy baseline than without, and weaker if it asserts an effect it cannot measure. Say what is not held.

5. Labour market and skills: commencements, guidance and one announcement without a document

5.1 Employment Rights Act: electronic and workplace balloting from 25 August 2026

The implementation timeline was updated on 7 August 2026 to confirm that electronic and workplace balloting for statutory trade union ballots takes effect on 25 August 2026. Narrow in itself, and the timeline page is the thing to watch rather than this measure: the phased commencements running through 2027 include the items with a real employer-cost dimension.

5.2 Youth Jobs Grant guidance reissued

DWP reissued the Youth Jobs Grant employer guidance on 7 August 2026, correcting errors, aligning it to the Find a Grant application form and moving the employer FAQ from PDF to HTML. Delivery detail rather than policy change, but the grant is the concrete £3,000 employer-facing offer identified in the July edition, so the current version is the one Business Gateshead communications should point at. Separately, DWP has given a provisional August 2026 date for a report titled Young People and Work: Rapid Attitudinal Polling — attitudinal evidence on the 18–24 cohort is thin and the team currently relies on administrative counts, so it is worth reading on publication. D-O2

5.3 14+ technical education pathways: still an announcement with no document

The 27 July 2026 announcement of 14+ technical education pathways, co-designed with mayors and with a rollout ambition from September 2028, remains exactly that — no white paper, no consultation, no new funding and no named co-design process. It is carried forward on the watch unchanged. The 2028 start sits after the North East AI and skills pilot window that closes in early 2027, so it is not a substitute for near-term action on the 18–24 cohort.

6. Evidence base: two items closed, one opened

6.1 Closed: the Transformed Labour Force Survey readiness assessment has a date

ONS publishes Labour market transformation — update on progress and plans on 11 August 2026, building on previous engagement on the Transformed Labour Force Survey and the labour market administrative data project. The July quarterly progress update reports improved response rates and fewer partial returns following the April design changes, and sets up an assessment of readiness to transition to the TLFS as the headline labour market measure in 2027. The July edition flagged a slip into 2027 as a live risk; 2027 is now the stated target, and the 11 August document is where the evidence for it is set out.

6.2 Closed: quarterly regional GDP returns, but not at a useful geography

ONS confirmed on 31 July 2026 that quarterly regional GDP estimates are being reinstated for England and Wales at ITL1 — the North East as a whole. No timetable was given for lower geographies. This closes the "waiting room" item carried in the July edition, and closes it unhelpfully: there is no Gateshead read, and the risk now is misreporting rather than absence.

6.3 New: the headline productivity statistic is being rebuilt, in the month our local figure lands

The same ONS improvement plan names, among its objectives, "moving in line with international best practice for productivity statistics by making the new 'component-based' approach our method underpinning our headline statistic". The covering note circulated to North region contacts places an explanatory article in September 2026 and migration in November 2026. Regional and subregional labour productivity for 2024 publishes on 5 November 2026. If the headline method changes in the same month the 2024 GVA-per-hour figure for Gateshead arrives, any comparison drawn against 2023 needs its basis stated explicitly.

Attribution caveat: the September and November timings come from the ONS Local North covering note circulated on 20 July 2026, not from the published improvement plan page, which names the change as an objective without dating it. Treat the dates as indicative until ONS publishes them.

Also new since the last edition. Regional gross disposable household income, 1997 to 2024 no longer carries a confirmed release date. As at 10 August the ONS release page reads "Provisional release date: August 2026"; a week earlier it read 19 August. It matters because it is the next new local income series.
