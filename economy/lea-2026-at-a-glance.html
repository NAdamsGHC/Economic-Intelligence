Gateshead's Economy at a Glance

The largest constraint on Gateshead's economy is the value of its work. Participation sits close to national levels but output per hour does not. Strategy should follow the value of work rather than the volume of it.

This page summarises the Gateshead Local Economic Assessment 2026, Interim Issue, September 2026. It is a provisional edition. Several major releases land between now and the end of the year, and the December 2026 edition will be the definitive one. Every figure below carries its own vintage, listed in full at the foot of the page.

Experimental analytical products, not official statistics. This is an independent, unofficial page and not a Gateshead Council publication. It is built from published official statistics, but the derived measures (ranks, decompositions, index positions and model results) are the author's analytical judgements. Three cautions apply throughout. Survey estimates for a single authority carry wide margins, so year-to-year movements can be unclear. Deprivation indices measure relative standing and cannot be compared across releases. The productivity modelling is associational, meaning it identifies what moves together with output rather than what causes it.

01Value Is Worth Around Three Times Volume

This is the central finding. Taking working-age population growth projected to 2043 and closing the entire employment-rate gap to England, which are deliberately generous assumptions, would put approximately 9,400 more residents into work. Reaching the UK average for output per hour on the job base the borough already has is worth around three times as much.

The case for raising participation rests on household income and on what work does for the people who gain it, rather than on the size of the output gain.
The read. Strategy should target the value the economy generates rather than the number of people in work. Participation is already close to national levels, so any strategy resting on participation gains has limited room.

02Output per Hour Is the Binding Constraint

Gateshead's gross value added per hour worked for 2023 is £32.16, against a North East median of £34.49 and an England median of £38.21 (ONS subregional productivity). That is 256th of 296 English authorities and 12th of the 15 areas ONS deems most similar. The gap has not closed at all in the past decade.

Where the Productivity Gap Sits

Share of the shortfall in value per job against England, decomposed
Valuing Gateshead's industry mix at England's rates accounts for around 4% of the shortfall in value per job. The remaining 96% is the performance of the sectors the borough already holds. Changing the industrial composition may raise the ceiling over a long horizon, but raising what existing firms produce addresses the rest.

03A Growth Gap Rather Than a Decline

Employee jobs across the seven Mayoral Strategic Authority areas rose by approximately 42,000 over the decade to 2024, while Gateshead's own count was flat. The borough's share of regional employment fell from 11.9% to 11.3%. Decomposing that shortfall against England shows where it comes from.

Industry mix accounts for a loss of around 1,500 jobs. The remaining 8,500 is the local component, being the amount by which Gateshead's own sectors underperformed the same sectors nationally. Gateshead produces less per employee job than England in twelve of fourteen sectors.

Adding Work Without Adding Value

Transport and storage against the borough average, 2024
Transport and storage employs approximately 7,760 people at around £28,800 of output per job, against a borough average of approximately £48,778, and it grew 63.0% between 2015 and 2024. Logistics is the borough's strongest market-sector employment specialism, at a location quotient of 1.61. This reconciles the borough adding work without adding value.

04Household Income Is the Weakest Single Position

Gross disposable household income per head for 2024 is £19,745, which is 274th of 296 English authorities and level with the bottom of Gateshead's fifteen statistical neighbours. Workplace pay sits closer to the national average than resident pay, so the borough hosts better-paid jobs than its residents typically hold.

The gap to the England median widened from 21.9% in 2023 to 22.1% in 2024. Household income spreads across everyone, whereas pay counts only those in work, which is why wages depict a slightly more positive story.

Resident and Workplace Pay, 2025

Workplace pay ranks 179th of 295 English authorities and resident pay 249th of 294. Workplace pay moved above resident pay in 2025, having previously been below it, implying that better-paid local jobs are held by people living elsewhere on average.

05Participation Is Close to National Levels

Employment and activity rates ran above the regional average and close to England through 2023 and 2024. The Jan–Dec 2025 survey year showed employment falling to 70.2%, but the following window recovers to 72.4%. On these margins the year-on-year fall is not statistically established, and the administrative data does not settle it either. The claimant count stood at 5,035 in July 2026, approximately 4.0% of residents aged 16–64, and on same-month comparisons the picture has levelled off.

06The Pipeline Is Stronger Than the Stock

Around 36.6% of working-age residents hold a Level 4 or higher qualification, against 48.0% in England. The skills gap matters most for what Gateshead residents earn rather than for what the borough produces, because qualified residents are recorded where they live but produce where they work. Beneath that stock, the post-16 pipeline is working.

Gateshead ranks 77th of the 152 authorities with education responsibilities. The borough achieves this with an unusually low "not known" rate of 0.6%, meaning it genuinely knows where its young people are, unlike many authorities where better tracking would likely raise the recorded figure. Apprenticeship achievements have risen from 690 in 2020/21 to 880 in 2024/25.

Five-Year Business Survival Rate, 2024

The 2019 cohort of business births still trading five years on
Five-year survival is above England, and the business birth rate ranks 97th of 296 English authorities. The weakness sits at the scale-up step rather than at formation. Information and communication has 45 sites employing 10 to 49 people and 5 employing 50 to 249, and has added sites of 10 or more employees faster than any other sector in the borough, up 42.9% since 2016 from a small base.

07A Place-Specific Gap Is Not a Fixed One

Our regression model finds that measured characteristics explain almost none of Gateshead's productivity gap against its statistical neighbours, leaving a large place-specific residual. Across 289 English authorities and fourteen years, no authority meaningfully changed the characteristics the model prices, so its variables should not be read as levers. A minority of authorities changed their trajectory anyway.

Productivity Index, Gateshead and Calderdale

GVA per hour worked, UK = 100. Calderdale is one of Gateshead's ONS statistical neighbours
Calderdale moved from an index of 78.3 in 2008 to 90.4 in 2021 while Gateshead fell from 85.3 to 78.5. Gateshead began seven points ahead and ended twelve points behind, a nineteen-point swing between two areas classed as alike, and the divergence began in 2011 for both. Calderdale has since declined again to 83.3 in 2023. Whatever produced that movement sits in the residual, which is what the model cannot see, and it is also where the room for local action sits.

08Four Priorities

The evidence resolves into four priorities, weighted towards the value of work because that is where the largest constraint sits.

Priority 1

Make Work in Gateshead Higher Value

Knowledge-intensive business services and manufacturing are associated with better local productivity values, and the borough is under-represented in professional, scientific and financial activities where output per hour is highest. Business support should favour industries where an additional job carries above-average value, where there is a local base at the scale-up threshold, and where the constraint is one the council can resolve. On those criteria construction, information and communication, and manufacturing would all be suitable.

Priority 2

Retain Scaling Firms Rather Than Feed Them Elsewhere

The borough has tended to hold a presence in sectors without sharing in their national growth, which aligns to the profile of a feeder. Information and communication is the clearest single case in the business counts. The pipeline is working but the step above it is not.

Priority 3

Reduce Economic Inactivity Through Health Pathways

Long-term sickness is the largest single reason for inactivity, at 33.0% of the inactive population or approximately 9,500 residents, which makes it the participation intervention with the most people to reach. Participation gains are worth around a third of what closing the productivity gap is worth, so this priority should be argued on household income and on what work does for the people who gain it.

Priority 4

Test Whether East–West Connectivity Constrains Access

The borough's persistent east–west divide in deprivation, health, skills and attainment is reinforced by a road network that separates rather than connects. Connectivity does not by itself raise productivity, but it determines which residents can reach which opportunities.

What would change this conclusion. Three things would move the position, being a sustained employment-rate decline significantly below regional and national averages confirmed by consecutive and statistically significant Annual Population Survey releases, sustained convergence in the productivity, output and household income gaps, and evidence that within-sector productivity gaps are closing.
