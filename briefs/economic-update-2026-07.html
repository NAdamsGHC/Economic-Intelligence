Quarterly Economic Update — July 2026

Nathaniel Adams, Economic Intelligence Analyst · published 3 August 2026

This is the web edition of the quarterly update circulated to the Economic Intelligence distribution list. The Quarterly Economic Update dashboard carries the full series behind everything below. Every figure here was pulled fresh from the official source on 3 August 2026.

The quarter in three lines

What changed. Both administrative series softened over the quarter. Gateshead's claimant count rose 60 to 5,090 in June 2026, up 1.2% on March, and payrolled employees fell 405 to 86,312 in May 2026, down 0.5% on February. Over the year both are lower, the claimant count by 90 and payrolled employees by 375. Not one of the four headline survey measures moved far enough to be called a change.

What it means. Our payroll fall over the year, at 0.4%, sits alongside 0.4% for the North East and 0.3% for England. Those gaps are smaller than the revisions this source makes between consecutive monthly editions, so no ranking should be read into them. The survey data can neither confirm nor contradict any of it, because every movement and every comparator gap sits inside Gateshead's confidence interval.

What to watch. Whether the claimant count's rise since December persists. It fell through 2025 to a low of 4,725 in December and is 365 higher now. Local authority claimant counts are published without seasonal adjustment, so part of a December-to-June rise is simply the time of year, and the year-on-year comparison is the one that controls for it. The August release gives the next reading.

Three notes on the table. England's payrolled employee figure is my own summation of the nine English regions, since ONS publishes the series for regions and local authorities but not for England as a single row; Gateshead and the North East are as published. Gateshead's payrolled employee figure is from the June 2026 edition of the tables and the comparators from the July 2026 edition, because local authority data updates only quarterly, and the two editions differ by around a tenth of a percent. The claimant count is classed as official statistics in development, because Universal Credit rollout changed who is counted, and it is not seasonally adjusted below national level. Claimant count and Annual Population Survey figures are from NOMIS; payrolled employees from the ONS and HMRC PAYE Real Time Information tables, seasonally adjusted.

Labour market

Gateshead's claimant count for June 2026 is 5,090, which is 4.0% of residents aged 16–64 (NOMIS, Claimant Count by sex and age). That is 60 higher than March and 90 lower than June 2025. The proportion moved within a tenth of a point across the quarter, at 4.0% in March, 3.9% in April and May, and 4.0% in June.

Where our proportion sits relative to the comparators is worth stating precisely, because previous editions have described it three different ways and none was right. Over the last fifteen months Gateshead has been above the North East in every single month, without exception. Against England we have been within two tenths of a point throughout, marginally above in three months, level in six and marginally below in six. So we are reliably above the region and, to all practical purposes, indistinguishable from England.

Two caveats belong with this series. It counts Jobseeker's Allowance claimants plus Universal Credit claimants required to search for work, and it is classed as official statistics in development because Universal Credit rollout changed who is included, with some in-work claimants now counted. It is also published without seasonal adjustment below national level, so month-to-month movements carry seasonal noise.

The survey picture is where care is needed. Gateshead's employment rate for those aged 16–64 for April 2025 to March 2026 is 72.4%, with a 95% confidence interval of plus or minus 4.2 percentage points (Annual Population Survey). That is 2.2 percentage points above last quarter's reading and 2.8 points below last year's, and both movements sit inside the interval. The same is true of the unemployment rate for those aged 16+ at 6.8% (±2.6pp), the economic inactivity rate for those aged 16–64 at 22.4% (±3.9pp), and the share of the economically inactive aged 16–64 who are long-term sick at 33.0% (±9.5pp). None of them moved detectably on the quarter or on the year, though the unemployment rate's 2.4 point rise on the year comes close to its threshold and is worth watching rather than dismissing.

Nor is Gateshead distinguishable from its comparators on any of the four. Our employment interval of 68.2% to 76.6% covers both the North East's 71.8% and England's 75.8%, and our unemployment interval of 4.2% to 9.4% covers both regional and national rates. That is a finding about how much a local sample of this size can tell us rather than a finding about our economy.

Payrolled employees carries no sampling error, because it counts rather than samples. Gateshead's payrolled employee count for May 2026 is 86,312 (ONS and HMRC PAYE Real Time Information, seasonally adjusted), having fallen in four of the last five months from 86,763 in January. We are down 0.5% on the quarter and 0.4% on the year, against 0.2% and 0.4% for the North East and 0.1% and 0.3% for England.

Those gaps are too small to rank, and the reason is instructive. Our figure comes from the June 2026 edition of the tables, because local authority data updates only quarterly and the July edition carries a stub in its place until 15 September. The comparators are from the July edition, which is the latest published. Between those two consecutive editions ONS revised the North East's May level by 0.08% and England's by 0.10%, which is the same order as the differences between the three areas' annual changes. Read the direction, which is consistently downward, and not the ordering.

Two further limits are worth naming. The series counts payrolled employees only and excludes the self-employed entirely, so it cannot speak to employment composition. And having no sampling error is not the same as being final, since recent months revise. For employability colleagues the practical point is that the softening is broad and national, so caseload pressure is more likely to arrive through sector and employer channels than through anything specific to Gateshead.

Standing local indicators

Pension-age claimants. No update this quarter. The NOMIS state pension claimant series we would normally use stopped being refreshed and its latest data is November 2018, so there is no current figure from a source we can pull without a licence key. Rather than quote an eight-year-old number with a current-sounding label, I am leaving this blank until we have a DWP Stat-Xplore key, which is free and is on the list.

Housing market. Gateshead's average house price for May 2026 is £158,765, up 6.3% on the year and down 0.5% on the month (HM Land Registry UK House Price Index). That annual rise is close to the North East's 5.9% and well ahead of England's 2.3%, on an average price that is a little over half England's £292,095. The first-time buyer average is £143,659, also up 6.3%. The level remains substantially below England's while the rate of increase is nearly three times it, so the gap is narrowing. For regeneration and housing colleagues, the rate is the thing to watch rather than the level, and it is the first-time buyer figure that will show any affordability effect first.

Cost of living

The CPIH annual rate for June 2026 is 2.8%, down from 3.4% in March (ONS consumer price inflation). CPI is 2.6%. Regular pay across the whole economy grew 3.4% in cash terms in the three months to May 2026, against CPIH averaging 3.1% over those same three months, so pay is ahead of prices by about a third of a percentage point. A year earlier the same comparison gave pay 5.0% against CPIH 3.8%, a margin of about 1.2 points. Pay is still winning, but by roughly a quarter of the margin it had twelve months ago.

Easing is not the same as over. The independent forecasts HM Treasury collates now put CPI at 3.2% by the fourth quarter of 2026, up from the 2.6% recorded in June, and that expectation has risen through the year, from 2.2% when the panel was asked in February. The current reading is a trough rather than a trend.

That matters more here than the national average implies, and the distributional evidence is now published rather than assumed. Households in the second income decile commit around 154 parts per thousand of their spending to food and non-alcoholic drinks and around 71 to electricity, gas and other fuels, against roughly 95 and 34 for the ninth decile (ONS, household spending by income decile, 2025). Poorer households therefore commit about twice the share of their spending to energy and around 1.6 times the share to food. Gateshead's income distribution sits below the England average, so a renewed rise concentrated in essentials lands harder on our residents than a national headline rate suggests.

For colleagues in welfare and advice services, the thing to plan for is not a sudden deterioration but a second tightening in the second half of the year, after a spring that will have felt like relief.

Business conditions and national context

UK GDP grew 0.6% in the first quarter of 2026 (ONS quarterly national accounts), a clear step up on the 0.1% recorded in each of the two preceding quarters. UK vacancies for April to June 2026 stand at 712,000, essentially flat on the previous three-month period and down from 734,000 in the November to January window, so the demand side has stabilised at a lower level rather than continuing to fall.

Producer input prices rose 7.3% in the year to June 2026 while factory gate prices rose 3.5% (ONS producer price inflation). Input costs running at more than twice output prices is a margin squeeze on manufacturers nationally. Whether it bites harder here depends on our manufacturing mix, which this edition does not measure, so it is flagged as a national signal to check against local intelligence rather than as a Gateshead finding.

The Business Insights and Conditions Survey for 6 to 19 July 2026 has 93% of businesses currently trading, with 84% fully trading and 10% partially. Thirteen per cent of trading businesses expect turnover to rise in August and the same 13% expect to raise their prices. Among businesses with 10 or more employees, cost of labour was the most reported challenge affecting turnover, at 36%, which sits consistently with the producer price picture. The S&P Global UK PMI for July is not yet final and so is not quoted here.

Notes

Sources and periods are given with each figure above. Three things worth holding in mind.

The Annual Population Survey reports rolling twelve-month windows, so a change on the quarter compares two windows sharing nine months of the same sample. That is why the series looks placid and then lurches, and why quarter-on-quarter movements should not be read as quarterly events.

The confidence intervals are intervals, not probabilities about the true value. Where the table says a movement is not a real change, it means the movement is smaller than the interval around the estimate, so we cannot tell it apart from sampling noise. That is not the same as saying nothing happened.

On the claimant proportion. It is a rate over an ONS population denominator, and new mid-2025 population estimates were published on 29 July 2026, after the claimant count was last refreshed on 21 July. Our 4.0% is on the denominator NOMIS held at the time of the pull and may be restated. The level of 5,090 is unaffected.
