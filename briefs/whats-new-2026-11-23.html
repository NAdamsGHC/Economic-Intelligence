17 November · DWP, No.10 and DfE

AmberThe Milburn Review Calls for a Youth Opportunity Service in Every Place, and the Government Backs Its Core

What changed?
The independent review of young people and work reported on 17 November 2026 (From a Welfare State to a Working State, a new settlement for the next generation). It says nearly one million young people, 1 in 8, are not in education, employment or training, that most are economically inactive rather than looking for work, and that six in 10 have never had a job. It sets a 10-year programme with an ambition to bring the NEET rate down to around 5% by 2036. Its own modelling of the proposals reaches around 6.8%.

The parts that land on places are these. A Youth Opportunity Service would operate in every part of England by 2029, built mainly from existing teams such as youth-facing Jobcentre Plus staff, local authority participation teams, Careers Hubs and Youth Hubs, and mayors could commission it locally. Strategic authorities would become responsible for the participation system for everyone aged 16 to 24, with local authorities remaining the local backbone and keeping their statutory duties for 16 and 17 year olds. Mayors would agree one Area Youth Participation Plan with their councils, the NHS, providers and employers, funded through multi-year Participation Agreements with government. Integrated care boards would get a legal duty to work with Mayoral Strategic Authorities on youth participation. The review also proposes a statutory Opportunity Guarantee and Support Guarantee, a single Youth Allowance in place of the Universal Credit health element and the Work Capability Assessment for under-25s, a £3,000 payment to employers hiring any young person who has been NEET for six months (with £2,000 more for small firms), and youth employment as a mandatory social value criterion in public contracts above £5 million where the contract creates meaningful labour market demand, with the social value framework extended to local government by law. It asks for £500 million in 2027-28 to start, found by reprioritising existing spending, and says investment should be a priority for the 2027 Spending Review.

The government said the same day that it agrees with the review’s core principles (DWP, Government commits to new settlement for young people). It committed to locally led youth services from next year, shaped by mayors and local leaders, an Opportunity and Support Guarantee, and a Youth Opportunities Unit reporting directly to the Prime Minister, which the review’s author will chair (Youth Opportunities Unit). The Work and Pensions Secretary told the Commons that, from April, the job guarantee will start after 12 months on Universal Credit rather than 18 (oral statement). The wider recommendations, including the benefit changes, will be considered with the Timms Review, with next steps on welfare reform in the coming weeks and a White Paper in the new year, followed by legislation. Local delivery, funding and legislation are to be set out in due course, and the Rewiring the State Delivery Plan will say how regional devolution supports it. The review page also invites councils to set up a local NEET Taskforce and employers to join First Chance.
Why it matters?
The review notes that the state’s responsibility for young people falls away after age 18, and its biggest change is to give that responsibility for 18 to 24 year olds to places. If it is taken forward, NEMSA would hold the 16 to 24 participation system for the borough, the council’s participation team would become part of a local Youth Opportunity Service, and funding would come through an agreement between government and NEMSA. Nothing changes yet. The government has backed the direction and the local delivery model, while the benefit changes wait for the White Paper. On fiscal devolution, the review says the plan to share local taxes should in time give mayors control of budgets and any savings, and that Participation Agreements should test places sharing the gains from preventing inactivity. No Spending Review detail on the retained tax share, no equalisation design and no Rewiring the State Delivery Plan were published in the window. This item draws on the government’s response and the review’s introduction and executive summary chapter, read in full. The other eight chapters and the analytical annex were searched for North East content. Beyond a Newcastle workshop and case study, and Gateshead Council’s name in the list of contributors, they carry none.
What should we do?
Nothing for us before the White Paper in the new year. Employment and skills colleagues will want to know about the NEET Taskforce invitation.
19 November · MHCLG

AmberHigh Street Rental Auction Funding Now Opens the Week of 7 December, Two Weeks Later

What changed?
MHCLG updated the High Street Rental Auctions Fund 2026 to 2028 prospectus on 19 November 2026 to say applications will open from the week commencing 7 December 2026. The 3 November edition had the week of 23 November, from the prospectus of 28 October. The rest is unchanged. Up to £10 million is awarded on a rolling basis, so the fund may close before its 31 January 2028 deadline. Councils can claim for vacancy registers, consumer market reports, property surveys and refurbishment of up to three premises at up to £200,000 each, with all activity finished by 31 March 2028. Why it matters?
Rental auctions let the council require a landlord to let a commercial unit that has been empty for long periods. Because money is awarded as applications arrive, the opening week matters more than the deadline.

5 November · Industrial Strategy Advisory Council, catch-up item

AmberThe Industrial Strategy Advisory Council’s First Annual Report Says Devolution Without Capability Could Widen Regional Gaps

What changed?
This is a catch-up item. The council published its annual report 2026 (78 pages) on 5 November 2026, inside the 9 November edition’s window, and no edition carried it, because gov.uk tagged it only to the council as an organisation, which the weekly log did not then query. The council advises the Chancellor and the Business Secretary on the Industrial Strategy. One year in, it says the strategy’s diagnosis still holds but delivery is too slow on the barriers businesses raise most, which are electricity prices, skills shortages, and planning and regulation. It asks for the Industrial Strategy to be the national framework for reindustrialisation, driven across departments by No.10 North and the National Economic Council. From year two it moves from tracking delivery to measuring outcomes, including training participation, skills gaps, apprenticeships, planning decision times, regional investment flows and AI adoption.

Its recommendations for places matter most locally. It says devolution of powers alone will not deliver growth, and that “devolution without capability risks increasing rather than reducing regional inequality”. Places need analytical, strategy, investment, spatial planning and delivery capability, and it asks government to define those capabilities and find the gaps. This repeats its July 2026 report on local economic capability, a study of Greater Manchester and the West Midlands.
Why it matters?
The report sets the national test that the borough’s growth and skills plans will be read against, and two of its recommendations land on strategic authorities.
  • Skills – Real-terms employer investment per trainee fell in every region and nation between 2011 and 2024, and the report’s map puts the North East’s fall in its smallest band, 0 to 10%, against more than 45% in London and the South East (ISAC analysis of the Employer Skills Survey 2024)
  • Further education – England’s 16 to 18 population is projected to rise by around 70,000 between 2025 and 2028, and colleges could need between 8,400 and 12,400 more teachers by 2028-29 than in 2020-21 (a DfE estimate the report quotes), which bears on post-16 places in the borough as skills functions move to strategic authorities
  • Cost of capital – it asks for a rapid review of regional differences in the cost of capital and for more public investment to go outside London and the South East, and it will monitor public investment flows by region
  • Supply chains – it wants supply chains mapped to foundational industries and local labour markets, with public procurement used to signal demand, which is relevant to business support and local procurement work
  • Energy costs – it asks government to consider expanding the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme as an interim measure, and notes the Cost Control Framework due at the 2027 Spending Review, which matters to the borough’s energy-intensive manufacturers
What should we do?
Nothing for colleagues now. The energy recommendations come back at the 2027 Spending Review, where the Cost Control Framework is due. The capability recommendations and the year-two monitoring list are a useful test for the economic strategy and the NEMSA growth plan.

20 November · No.10 and Cabinet Office

GreenUK-EU Summit Deals Would Link Carbon Markets From 2028 and Exempt Steel Exports From the EU Carbon Border Tax

What changed?
The Prime Minister agreed a package with the EU at the second UK-EU Summit in Brussels on 20 November 2026 (No.10, Prime Minister secures deal with EU, and the key documents). Linking the UK and EU emissions trading schemes would exempt £6.5 billion of British exports, including steel and aluminium, from the EU’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism. Legislation is due in 2027, and the link is meant to apply from 1 January 2028. A food and drink agreement would end most checks and paperwork on food, farming and plant products, including Export Health Certificates, from 1 March 2028, subject to UK and EU procedures, through a European Partnership Bill. A Youth Experience Scheme would let 18 to 31 year olds live, work and study in the EU for up to three years, capped and visa-based, with the first young people travelling no later than January 2030. The government says the deals together could add up to 0.5% to UK GDP by 2040.
Why it matters?
The link would take steel and aluminium exports out of the EU’s carbon border tax from 2028, which removes a cost for manufacturers that sell metals into the EU. Food producers and wholesalers exporting to the EU would save on certificates and checks. None of the figures is broken down by region.
What should we do?
Nothing for us. Business support colleagues may want the dates for exporters.

19 November · ONS

GreenBusiness Births in Gateshead Rose to 750 in 2025 as Deaths Fell to 610

What changed?
ONS published Business demography, UK: 2025 on 19 November 2026. Gateshead’s count of births of new enterprises for 2025 is 750, up from 700 in 2024, and its count of deaths of enterprises for 2025 is 610, down from 630. Its count of active enterprises for 2025 is 6,215, up from 6,120 (Business demography reference tables 1.1c, 2.1c and 3.1c, counts rounded to the nearest 5). That gives Gateshead a birth rate of approximately 12.1% and a death rate of approximately 9.8% (our calculation from the published counts). The UK birth rate was 11.3% and the death rate 9.4%, the lowest since 2006. The North East and West Midlands had the joint-highest death rate of any region, at 10.1%.
Why it matters?
More firms started and fewer closed in Gateshead in 2025, and the stock of active businesses grew by about 95. This is the Business Demography base of 6,215. UK Business Counts, which counts businesses registered for VAT or PAYE at a point in March, gives a different and lower figure, and the two should not be mixed.
What should we do?
Nothing for us.
17 November · ONS

GreenONS Changes Its Headline Measure of Productivity

What changed?
ONS published Labour productivity, UK: July to September 2026 on 17 November 2026. From this release the component approach is the headline measure of labour productivity, replacing estimates based on Pay As You Earn Real Time Information, and whole economy figures are revised back to 1997. There is no longer a separate flash and overview estimate. The UK’s output per hour for July to September 2026 was 2.5% higher than a year earlier, and output per worker 2.0% higher. Industry revisions for 1997 to 2024 follow early next year. The bulletin does not say when local estimates move to the new basis.
Why it matters?
The change alters how hours worked are counted, so output per hour figures on the old and new bases cannot be compared. Output itself is unchanged. The subregional productivity release for 2024 lands tomorrow, 24 November.
What should we do?
Nothing for colleagues.

GreenInflation Rose to 3.6% in October

What changed?
ONS reported on 18 November 2026 that the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) rose by 3.6% in the 12 months to October 2026, up from 3.5% in September, and CPIH by 3.7% (Consumer price inflation, UK: October 2026). Motor fuels made the largest upward contribution, and restaurants and hotels the largest downward one. Core CPI, which leaves out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, rose by 2.8%.
Why it matters?
Inflation is still well above the 2% target, and fuel pushed it up in October, which squeezes real pay for Gateshead households. The next figure lands on 16 December, the day before the Bank of England’s December rate decision.

17 to 18 November · ONS

GreenGateshead’s Claimant Count Rose to 5,035 in October

What changed?
Gateshead’s claimant count for October 2026 is 5,035, which is 4.0% of residents aged 16 to 64 (ONS claimant count, via NOMIS, published in UK labour market: November 2026 on 17 November). The rate is 3.7% across the North East and 4.1% in England. September has been revised to 4,940 (3.9%), from the 5,050 first published, so the October count is up by 95 on the revised figure. ONS also published Private rent and house prices, UK: November 2026 on 18 November, which carries figures for Gateshead.
Why it matters?
The claimant count is an administrative count with no sampling interval, so the monthly change is a real change in claims, although first estimates are revised. Gateshead sits above the North East rate and just below England’s.

16 November · HM Treasury

GreenFace-to-Face Banking to Be Protected in Law

What changed?
HM Treasury published the independent Review of Access to Banking Services on 16 November 2026 and accepted its central recommendation to legislate to protect access to in-person banking (HM Treasury, Government commits to safeguard face-to-face banking). The review found more than three million adults regularly rely on banking in person, and that declining access has caused widespread harm. A statutory framework will be designed with the Financial Conduct Authority and industry, including what counts as reasonable access. No dates are given.
Why it matters?
Bank branches and banking hubs bring people into town and district centres, and small businesses that handle cash depend on them. The framework could give the council more to point to when a branch closes, but its design is not yet known.
What should we do?
Nothing for us.
