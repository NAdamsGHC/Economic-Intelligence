What's New · weekly round-up · covering 31 August – 8 September

What's New, 8 September 2026

A short weekly read of what's coming, what's landed and what matters for Gateshead's economy. Each item says why it's here. The Monday edition ran a day late, so this is a catch-up covering the nine days since the last one rather than the usual seven. Two things carry the week. The borough has a local NEET rate for the first time, from a one-off Census based release, and a busy few days at the centre left the fiscal devolution mechanism exactly where it was while naming the North East in three separate announcements. There is also a correction to make and a fault in our own collection to report.

Six weeks on, the fiscal devolution mechanism is still unpublished, and the North East was named three times in a single speech. The Chancellor's growth speech of 7 September restated the design set out in July, that there would be "greater business rates retention for local councils and strategic authorities" and "grants from central government replaced by a share of local income tax for every Mayoral Strategic Authority beginning in 2028" (HM Treasury, Chancellor's Growth Speech 2026, delivered at the Manufacturing Technology Centre, 7 September 2026). That substitution and that date were already public in the Cabinet Statement of 31 July, so the speech confirms rather than advances them. Every number that decides whether the swap helps or harms this borough is still missing. There is no percentage, no baseline, no reset period, no equalisation formula and no list of the grants to be abolished, and the Chancellor said "at the Budget I will set out a roadmap to fiscal devolution", which keeps all of it behind 28 October. What did move is who the money is going to. The North East was named as a new National Wealth Fund Strategic Partner, and two further Northern instruments were announced, all of them at strategic authority level and none with a borough split.

The Local Numbers

Wed 2 Sep · ONS · new local measure

Gateshead has a local NEET rate for the first time, and both sexes sit in the worst sixth of England

Why it matters: Gateshead's model estimated age-standardised percentage of young people aged 16 to 24 who were not in education, employment or training on Census Day, 21 March 2021 is 15.77% for females (95% confidence limits 14.94% to 16.60%) and 17.49% for males (16.61% to 18.36%) (ONS, Geographical variation in the prevalence of young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), England: March 2021, released 2 September 2026). The underlying counts are 8,610 young women in the study population of whom 1,385 were NEET, and 8,720 young men of whom 1,545 were NEET, rounded by the ONS to the nearest five. Until this release there was no local authority NEET measure covering the full 16 to 24 age band at all. The only local figure available was the Department for Education's 16 to 17 series, and the national 18 to 24 picture came from a survey that is not published below UK level.

Our own ranking of the published figures puts Gateshead 24th highest of 150 upper tier local authorities for females and 20th highest of 150 for males. The highest in England is Blackpool, at 22.2% for males and 20.4% for females. The lowest are Bath and North East Somerset for males at 6.1% and York for females at 4.6%.

Inside the North East the borough is mid-table, and almost none of the differences are measurable. Gateshead is the fourth lowest of the twelve North East upper tier authorities for females and the sixth lowest for males. The confidence limits overlap with every other North East authority except Newcastle upon Tyne and County Durham, so those two are the only differences in the region that can be called established rather than apparent. The Newcastle comparison is the one to leave alone. The ONS warns in its own notes that authorities associated with university towns and cities take in students who are counted at their term-time address, which masks NEET prevalence among the young people who grew up there. That works in both directions across the Tyne. Newcastle's 7.21% and 8.38% are the lowest in the region by a wide margin, and Gateshead's own 18 to 21 year olds studying elsewhere are counted at their term-time address and removed from this denominator as students. Gateshead against Newcastle on this measure is not like for like and should not be presented as one.

Figure 1: Age-Standardised NEET Percentage by Sex, North East Upper Tier Local Authorities. Source: ONS, Geographical variation in the prevalence of young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), England: March 2021, table 4, Census Day 21 March 2021.

The modelling is where the useful finding sits. The ONS publishes incidence rate ratios against a reference authority, Bath and North East Somerset, adding explanatory factors one layer at a time. On the baseline model Gateshead's young women are 3.0606 times as likely to be NEET (2.8205 to 3.3212) and young men 2.8594 times (2.6526 to 3.0824). Adding living arrangements, individual characteristics and then area deprivation pulls both down a long way, and on the full area model the ratios are 1.2678 for females (1.1687 to 1.3752) and 1.2686 for males (1.1752 to 1.3695). Both remain statistically significant. So most of the gap between this borough and the best performing authority in England is associated with who lives here and how deprived the area is, and an excess of approximately 27% survives after all of it is accounted for. That residual is the part that is not explained by composition, and it is the part policy would have to be aimed at.

Four limits belong with any use of this figure, and they are severe. It is a snapshot of one day, 21 March 2021, taken during the pandemic, and the ONS asks users to consider that when interpreting it. It is not a series and will not be repeated. It is published at upper tier local authority level only, so there is no ward or neighbourhood NEET rate in it; the lower level tables in the workbook are input variables from other sources, not NEET outcomes. And it does not connect to either of the measures we already use. The ONS states that the estimates "are not comparable with our official headline UK estimates of young people who were NEET using Labour Force Survey data" and "are also not comparable to the official estimates of young people who were NEET in England produced by the Department for Education". It covers the whole 16 to 24 band and cannot be split by age, so it does not test our own finding that the borough is strong at 16 to 17 and exposed at 18 to 24. What it does is give the whole band a local number for the first time, with an interval around it.

Wed 2 Sep · DWP · new local figures, then discontinued

Housing Benefit overpayments identified in Gateshead more than halved through the year, in the last edition of the series

Why it matters: Gateshead's total value of Housing Benefit overpayments outstanding at the start of the quarter for January to March 2026 is £3,113 thousand, made up of £1,535 thousand rent rebate and £1,577 thousand rent allowance (DWP, Housing Benefit Debt Recoveries statistics: April 2025 to March 2026, released 2 September 2026). The stock fell steadily across the year, from £3,366 thousand at the start of April to June 2025. The flow fell faster. Overpayments identified during the quarter were £397 thousand in April to June 2025, £358 thousand in July to September, £235 thousand in October to December and £180 thousand in January to March 2026, so the borough identified less than half as much in the final quarter as in the first. Recoveries held up far better, at £376 thousand, £332 thousand, £238 thousand and £237 thousand. Write-offs were £119 thousand, £72 thousand, £107 thousand and £86 thousand. Across the four quarters that is approximately £1.17 million identified and approximately £1.18 million recovered.

This is the last edition, so the measure stops here. The workbook's guidance sheet states that the department "has considered the feedback received during the recent public consultation on the value and frequency of the HBDR publication" and that "Feedback did not identify an ongoing public need for this information to be published". These were accredited official statistics published at local authority level, and there is no successor named. A quarterly administrative series showing what a council identifies, recovers and writes off on housing benefit debt is not something we can rebuild from another source, so the sensible step is to take a copy of the full local time series now rather than after the page is archived. On the substance, a halving of identified overpayments inside one year is more likely to reflect what the authority was able to process than a change in the underlying rate of overpayment, and nothing published here separates those two.

The Centre of Government

Mon 7 Sep · HM Treasury · fiscal devolution

The North East is named in three of the Chancellor's announcements, and all three land above the borough

Why it matters: alongside the restated fiscal devolution design, the growth speech of 7 September carried three things that are genuinely new and that name this region. The Chancellor announced that "South Yorkshire, Liverpool City Region, North-East England and Cardiff Capital Region will all become the latest Strategic Partners of the National Wealth Fund", "a new allocation from the British Business Bank, £150m for scale-ups in the North", and "the Northern 500: bringing the North's most ambitious mid-sized businesses into a single growth community", described in the accompanying release as led by the Great North partnership of mayors (HM Treasury, 7 September 2026). The same release commits to cutting the regulatory administrative burden by 25%, a fixed challenge window for legal challenges to infrastructure, and a Regulating for Growth Bill "ready next year".

All three land at the strategic authority, and none carries a published local split. That is now the third set of announcements in a fortnight with that shape, after the Social and Affordable Homes Programme and the Rough Sleeping Programme. The pattern is worth stating once rather than noting each time. Money and function increasingly arrive at the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority, and the borough's share is settled inside the combined authority's own programme rather than by a central allocation we can read off. What can be prepared in advance is the evidence for that internal conversation. Becoming a National Wealth Fund Strategic Partner means the region builds its own investment pipeline, which makes borough site and sector evidence the currency. For the Northern 500 and the scale-up allocation, the question is how many Gateshead firms sit in the mid-sized band those schemes target, which is answerable now from UK Business Counts by employment size band. Neither scheme has published eligibility criteria.

Mon 7 Sep · HM Treasury, Cabinet Office and Attorney General · machinery

The default on consultation has been reversed, which narrows the main route this team has into central government

Why it matters: a joint letter to all ministers from the Chancellor, the First Secretary of State and the Attorney General states that "there is no general duty of consultation, and that the default position should be that ministers reach decisions and proceed to act on them" (HM Treasury, The Simplification and Agency of Government: letter to all ministers, 7 September 2026). Formal consultation is to be reserved for three cases: where it is required by statute, where it would be conspicuously unfair not to consult, or where ministers judge that outside input is warranted. The letter calls habitual consultation "a distraction to government", asks departments to find consultation and reporting requirements that could be repealed in future bills, and sets the starting point that "requirements should be repealed, retained only by exception". It also tells ministers they may proceed where "there is a tenable legal argument" even at high legal risk, and signals an extension of the judicial review limits from energy projects to all nationally significant infrastructure.

This is a machinery change with a direct effect on how this council influences policy. The Deadlines Running Now table below is a list of consultations, and consultation responses are the main formal route by which evidence from this borough reaches a department. If the volume of consultation falls, that route narrows, and the value of the ones that do open rises accordingly. Two practical consequences follow. The open consultations already in the diary, particularly the mayoral planning powers one closing on 5 October, matter more than they did a week ago because there may be fewer of them. And the trailed high streets regulatory package, which the government has repeatedly said "will be consulted on shortly", now sits under a policy that says departments should consult less. It is worth reading that commitment as less secure than it was.

Mon 7 Sep · MHCLG · tier of government

Local government reorganisation has been paused across England, and four decisions already taken have been withdrawn

Why it matters: the government has withdrawn its reorganisation decisions for Essex, Southend-on-Sea and Thurrock; Hampshire, Isle of Wight, Portsmouth and Southampton; Norfolk; and Suffolk, and paused the rest of the programme (MHCLG, Review of local government reorganisation launched, 7 September 2026, and the accompanying letter to council leaders). The stated reason is legal: "Following updated and privileged legal advice, ministers will review plans for Local Government Reorganisation." The review covers the decisions taken in July 2026 and the two areas still awaiting a decision. "Plans for 14 other areas, along with Cambridgeshire & Peterborough and West Sussex, are also being reviewed and paused while this is carried out." Surrey is unaffected. The government has confirmed "that local elections in May 2027 will go ahead on existing council boundaries". No completion date for the review is given.

Gateshead is not being reorganised, and that is exactly why this matters here. The borough is already a metropolitan district and none of the paused areas is in the North East, so there is no direct structural consequence. The consequence is to the timetable everything else sits on. The same release says "The government's Rewiring the State White Paper on devolution will also set out plans to rewire the country and ensure effective and sustainable local government in future", which is the first time that white paper has been named on gov.uk in that form. It is due with the Budget on 28 October, and it is now carrying the answer to a reorganisation programme that has been stopped on legal advice as well as the fiscal devolution roadmap. A white paper with more to settle and the same publication date is a white paper more likely to defer things, and the equalisation design is the item this borough cannot afford to see deferred again.

Wed 2 Sep · Cabinet Office

Three cross-government taskforces set up inside the centre, one of them on community regeneration

Why it matters: the Prime Minister has established three cross-government taskforces within the Office for the Prime Minister and Cabinet, on public control, community regeneration and cost of living (Cabinet Office, 2 September 2026). The release commits them to "Work across central and local government - including with regional Mayors as the government continues to pass powers from Westminster", and frames the regeneration strand around visible decline, with the Prime Minister quoted on "High streets falling into disrepair, rubbish piling up at the sides of our streets - we will reverse that". No funding, membership, terms of reference or timetable is published with it.

It is named here because it is a centre structure rather than because it changes anything yet. Two of the three themes are ones this team already holds evidence on. The high streets and local centres work is directly in scope of a community regeneration taskforce, and the cost-of-living index sits under the third. An announcement with no document behind it is not something to act on, but a taskforce inside the centre is where a high streets ask would eventually have to land, and it is worth knowing it now exists.

Tue 1 Sep · Prime Minister's Office

A mayoral overnight visitor levy is named as the first fiscal power, and it commences later than we had it

Why it matters: in a statement to the House of Commons the Prime Minister said "This Government will oversee the biggest redistribution of power this country has seen" and "I today extend the same offer to all parts of England without devolved power" (Prime Minister's Office, PM statement to the House of Commons, 1 September 2026). The statement also described Number 10 North as "The single front door on which regions can knock to get quicker joined-up decisions from Whitehall", and restated the VAT cut on electricity bills and the business rates cut for pubs, social clubs and live music venues.

The levy is the detail that matters, because it is the first fiscal devolution power with a commencement attached and the date has moved. The statement describes the offer as including "powers long requested by mayors such as post-16 technical education, employment support and fiscal devolution, starting with the ability to set an overnight visitor levy to commence towards the end of the financial year 27-28". The team's standing record of the Cabinet Statement has the levy available to strategic authorities from April 2027, with revenue plans due by March 2028. Towards the end of 2027/28 is later than April 2027. The two can be reconciled, because a power can exist from one date and be exercised from another, but nothing published says which reading is right and the difference is a full year of revenue. It is recorded here as a discrepancy rather than resolved. Either way this is a North East Mayoral Strategic Authority power with a direct Gateshead incidence through the Quayside accommodation stock, and the borough has no baseline for overnight stays or accommodation supply. That baseline should exist before the design consultation opens, not after.

The offer itself is not addressed to us, and the read there is about dilution rather than opportunity. Extending the same offer everywhere means more strategic authorities competing for the same functions and the same finite pot, and the established authorities lose relative position as the number of them grows. That interacts directly with the income tax share announced six days later, because a share of a local tax base is worth less to a weak base the wider the scheme is spread and the thinner the equalisation.

Why it matters: the government has launched a Sovereign AI R&D Procurement Scheme worth £100 million, open to British AI start-ups with demonstrator-stage technology, with the state acting as first customer and successful firms keeping the intellectual property they create (Cabinet Office and HM Treasury, 31 August 2026). Policy and National Data

Wed 2 Sep · ONS · national data

Nearly a third of England's NEET young people were on no benefits at all, which is the cleanest reason yet to distrust the claimant proxy

Why it matters: the companion release to the geography bulletin above finds that "Nearly one-third of young people who were not in education, employment or training (NEET) (32.8%) in England on Census Day… were not recorded as receiving benefits", at 37.9% of males and 27.1% of females (ONS, Characteristics of young people not in education, employment or training (NEET) who did not receive benefits, England: March 2021, released 2 September 2026). Those without benefits were in much better health, with 61.0% reporting very good health against 39.1% of those receiving benefits, and lived in much better off households, with average household earnings of £44,600 against £24,253. Two thirds of them, 67.0%, had recorded employment at some point. This is England only, with no regional or local breakdown, and no confidence intervals are published.

It puts a number on a caveat we have been carrying without one. The 18 to 24 claimant count is the only frequent local proxy the team has for youth worklessness, and we have always said it understates the position because not every NEET young person claims. This says that on Census Day roughly a third of them were on nothing at all, and that the third in question is healthier and better off than those claiming. The practical consequence is that the claimant proxy is not simply too low, it is skewed, and it misses the part of the cohort least visible to any service. The borough's 18 to 24 claimant count for July 2026 was 990, and nothing here lets us scale it, but it does say what direction the error runs in.

Thu 3 Sep · ONS · the register under our business data

The register that our business counts are built on is being replaced, and the regulator says the hard part is still ahead

Why it matters: the ONS has responded to an Office for Statistics Regulation compliance review of the Statistical Business Register, the successor to the Inter-Departmental Business Register (ONS letter of response, 3 September 2026). The regulator recognised "stronger governance, clearer collaboration between delivery partners and a more credible phased delivery approach" after a reset, but said "the most significant delivery challenges remain ahead". The ONS has committed to an executive sponsor, "clear quality criteria and success measures for each transition state", and better reporting on delivery confidence, with a phased transition ending in the old register being retired.

The Inter-Departmental Business Register is the sampling frame and the source behind almost everything we publish about the business base. The Business Register and Employment Survey, UK Business Counts and business demography all sit on it. Nothing published today changes any figure, and this is context rather than a correction. What it does mean is that the business base half of the evidence is going to be rebuilt underneath us over the next few years, and that discontinuities are more likely than usual when they arrive. It is worth holding a note of which of our products depend on it, because that is the list that will need re-checking at each transition.

Wed 2 Sep · ONS · national data

Working and workless households published for the UK only

Why it matters: the quarterly estimates of working and workless households for April to June 2026 are published at UK level in this bulletin (ONS, Working and workless households in the UK: April to June 2026, released 2 September 2026). Household level worklessness is the measure that shows what the individual level claimant count cannot, which is how joblessness concentrates within households, and it feeds the inactivity picture. There is no regional or local detail in this edition. Fri 4 Sep · ONS · methods

Travel and tourism statistics corrected and rebuilt, with still no local authority output

Why it matters: the ONS has published corrections to airport code assignment affecting the third and fourth quarters of 2024 and the 2024 annual estimates, corrections to currency conversion for package holiday costs from July 2024 to June 2025, and a move to a two-partition calibration approach based on departure airport and destination country (ONS, Improving our travel and tourism statistics: September 2026 update, released 4 September 2026). Great Britain Tourism Survey country groupings have been realigned and North America split into Canada and the United States. These remain official statistics in development. The next update is due in October 2026 alongside the 2025 estimates, which will give six quarters of comparable data under the new survey design.

No local authority output is announced or planned. That confirms the position the team already holds, which is that there is no ONS sub-regional measure of tourism value, and the visitor economy objectives have to be evidenced from other sources. This is worth restating because the visitor objectives are written in terms of measurable increases, and the measure does not exist at this geography.

Our Own Record

We missed a Gateshead funding allocation, and here it is. The Rough Sleeping Programme was published on 19 August, inside the window the 25 August edition covered, and it was not reported. It carries a spreadsheet of allocations by authority. Gateshead's total allocation is £503,660.90 across the three years 2026/27 to 2028/29, all of it revenue and none of it capital, made up of £325,182.90 in 2026/27, £103,731.56 in 2027/28 and £74,746.43 in 2028/29 (MHCLG, Rough Sleeping Programme, 19 August 2026). The programme total is £442 million over three years. The seven North East authorities' original allocations sum to £6,891,287.44, which matches the strategic authority row in the same workbook exactly, and the seven no longer appear in the current authority sheet because the money now routes through the combined authority. So the borough figure is an indicative share rather than an allocation the council receives directly. The workbook labels that row "North East Combined Authority", which is the old name for the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority, and it is quoted here as published.
The departmental log has been under-counting badly, and the cause is in our own mailroom. The log below is built from a direct check of the four subscribed departments on gov.uk rather than from the mailroom digest alone, and the two do not agree. The digest carried 49 items for this window. The direct check found 148. The gap is not a change in government activity. The mailroom script truncates any single email that exceeds a size limit, and the weekly Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government and Department for Education emails are long enough to hit it, so both were cut off partway through their item lists. Every previous edition's log was reconciled against the digest and would have agreed with itself while missing the same items. The reconciliation test we have been running proves the digest was transcribed faithfully, not that the digest was complete. The most consequential single item lost this way was the reorganisation review written up above, which the mailroom never carried. The log from this edition onward is built from source, and the digest is used for the change notes it does carry. The truncation itself needs fixing in check_mail.py.
The department abbreviates itself BIST, not DBIST. The 31 August edition switched the log to DBIST after seeing the new name on a publication. On 7 September the department updated its own sponsorship guidance, which is titled "Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST) sponsorship" and uses BIST throughout (gov.uk, 7 September 2026). The page is attributed to both the old and the new organisation, so the transition is still in progress and the subscribed feed still arrives under the old name. Our abbreviation should follow the department's own, and the log will switch to BIST next week rather than mid-edition.
The high streets written evidence deadline passed last night. The call for written evidence to the Revitalising High Streets inquiry closed at 11.59pm on Monday 7 September. It has been the binding date in the diary since 17 August and it is now gone. The trailed high streets regulatory package, covering the vape shop use class, the revocation of the Gambling Act "aim to permit" duty and adult gaming centre planning permission, was checked again for this edition and has still not opened. That is four consecutive weeks. The consultation default letter of 7 September makes that delay more material than it was, because the commitment to consult "shortly" now sits under an instruction to consult less. The baselining action flagged on 17 August, counting vape, betting and adult gaming centre hereditaments across the borough's local centres before the use class change removes the counterfactual, is unaffected by any of this and is still outstanding.
The Jobs Guarantee question from 31 August has an answer, and it is a bad one for this borough. The 31 August edition reported that the Jobs Guarantee had made its first starts on 26 and 28 August with no participant numbers and no regional cut published. The regional cut is in the release and was missed. Phase one runs in Birmingham and Solihull, the East Midlands, Greater Manchester, Hertfordshire and Essex, central and east Scotland, and south east and south west Wales. The North East is not in it. The offer is up to 25 hours a week of paid work for six months, fully government funded, for 18 to 24 year olds who have been on Universal Credit for 18 months or more, with a target of more than 90,000 people supported by 2029 and national expansion promised later in 2026. This is the sharpest finding of the sweep. The national instrument aimed precisely at the cohort where this borough is most exposed has started everywhere except here, and the borough's own 18 to 24 claimant count of 990 in July 2026 was up 8.8% on June 2025. That is an evidenced ask to make of the department before expansion is designed rather than after. Figures circulating on phase one referral volumes come from trade press rather than from the department and are not used here.
Nothing arrived in the mailroom that asked for anything. Sixty-nine messages were read for this edition. Two were forwarded in, a visitor economy roundup and a corporate policy digest for September, both of which are treated as reading rather than as taskings. The policy digest pointed to the Pride in Place Plans guidance and the Rough Sleeping Programme, and the second of those is the correction recorded above, so a forwarded item caught something our own scan had missed. Nothing in the inbox is treated as a tasking.

A week that gave the borough a number it has never had, and took a measure away. The ONS published a local NEET rate for 16 to 24 year olds for the first time, and Gateshead sits at 15.77% for young women and 17.49% for young men on Census Day 2021, both in the worst sixth of England. The modelling underneath it says most of the gap to the best performing authority is associated with who lives here and how deprived the area is, and that an excess of approximately 27% remains after all of that is accounted for. It is a one-off, mid-pandemic, and it does not join up with either measure we already use, so it is a baseline rather than a trend. In the same morning's releases, nearly a third of England's NEET young people were found to be receiving no benefits at all, which is the clearest published reason yet to treat the youth claimant count as a skewed proxy rather than simply a low one. Against that, the Housing Benefit debt recoveries series was published for the last time, so a quarterly local authority measure disappears. At the centre, three funding announcements in a fortnight have now landed at the strategic authority with no borough split, the reorganisation programme has been stopped on legal advice with the white paper due in seven weeks, and departments have been told to consult less, which narrows the route this team uses to put local evidence in front of government. The fiscal mechanism itself has not moved in six weeks. Two things in this edition are our own: a Gateshead funding allocation we failed to report in August, corrected above with the figure, and a fault in the mailroom that has been silently cutting the departmental log by roughly two thirds.
