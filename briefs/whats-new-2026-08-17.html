What's New · weekly round-up · covering 10 – 17 August

What's New — 17 August 2026

A short weekly read of what's coming, what's landed and what matters for Gateshead's economy. Each item says why it's here. No local statistics landed again this week, but two things happened that outrank a normal data week. The statistical status of the survey behind every Gateshead employment figure was settled, and not in the direction we were expecting. And a national high streets package landed that points straight at the priority centres, four weeks before the select committee inquiry closes to evidence.

The labour market numbers have changed status. On 11 August the ONS wrote to the Office for Statistics Regulation to confirm that Labour Force Survey and Annual Population Survey outputs move from "official statistics in development" to "official statistics", and that it "will not be seeking reaccreditation for LFS and APS-derived outputs". Regional gross disposable household income is the single exception and will be considered for reaccreditation separately, because APS is one input among several rather than the dominant source. The Annual Population Survey is where the borough's employment rate, economic inactivity rate and qualification levels come from. Those figures are official statistics, they are not accredited official statistics, and as of 11 August that is a settled position rather than a transitional one. Every product that describes them otherwise needs its assurance wording corrected.

Landed this week

Tue 11 Aug

Labour market transformation, and the designation of LFS and APS outputs

Why it matters: two documents, one consequential. The transformation update reports the outcome of the first Transformed Labour Force Survey readiness assessment, which concluded that "now is not the right time to transition" — only one quarter of data reflects the latest survey design, which is too little to judge. The LFS therefore "remains the lead source of labour market data". The revised route is a progress check and a detailed transition plan in January 2027, a decision point in July 2027, and transition planned for November 2027. That is later than the 2027 headline transition we have been caveating against, and it means the survey behind our confidence intervals does not change this year or next.

The harder half is the accompanying letter to the regulator. Alongside the designation change, the ONS names "reductions in the sample boost and interviewer numbers following ONS prioritisation decisions" as an additional quality consideration specific to the Annual Population Survey, and commits to "further advice on the quality of the APS when the next dataset is released in September 2026". A reduced sample boost bites hardest at local authority level, which is the only level we use it at. The September quality article is the thing to read properly when it appears — it should tell us how much of the Gateshead labour market picture survives a thinner sample.

Tue 11 Aug

High street package — vape shops, betting shops and adult gaming centres

Why it matters: this is the first national lever aimed squarely at the composition problem in local centres rather than at vacancy or footfall in the abstract. Vape shops are to require planning permission, with the definition tightened so that premises cannot avoid it by presenting as convenience stores, and rules to prevent opening near schools. Adult gaming centres are to require planning permission, with the changes due to take effect at the start of 2027. The government intends to revoke the Gambling Act's "aim to permit" duty, which currently requires licensing authorities to work towards permitting gambling. Maximum closure orders double from six to 12 months. There is also to be clearer national expectation on shopfront design, with practical guidance for councils. The release cites nearly 1,800 pubs and bars lost against vape and tobacco shops rising to almost 2,200 since 2016.

What follows from it: the measures are announcements, not law — nothing has been consulted on yet, and no consultation had opened as at today. The useful work is baseline work, and it has a deadline attached to it. Once the use-class change takes effect there is no way back to a clean before-and-after, so a count of vape, betting and adult gaming premises across the borough's local centres needs taking on the current position rather than after the fact. It also gives the high streets inquiry submission a live government position to respond to instead of an open question, with three weeks to use it.

Fri 14 Aug

Community and Engagement Survey 2025/26 — local authority breakdowns added

Why it matters: the only genuinely new local data in the window. The survey was first published on 31 July, but the geographic breakdowns were added on 14 August, and the publication states it carries national, regional and local authority level data for England covering October 2025 to March 2026. Topics include pride in local area, neighbourhood, volunteering, support networks, loneliness, libraries and influence over local decisions. Pride in local area is the stated outcome measure behind the Pride in Place programme, and there is currently no published third-party baseline for it here.

Read it before relying on it. This is a national survey being cut to local authority level over a six-month field period, which is exactly the shape of estimate that turns out to be too thin to track change. Before anything is built on it, the local authority base sizes need checking, along with whether the estimates are direct or modelled and whether confidence intervals are published. If the bases do not hold, that is a finding worth stating rather than a reason to say nothing. Note also that this survey replaced both the Participation Survey and the Community Life Survey in October 2025, so any series held on either is discontinued at that break.

Fri 14 Aug

TechFirst AI apprenticeship pathway — first bootcamp, north-west only

Why it matters: a three-week summer bootcamp for up to 70 people aged 16 to 21 in Wigan, Blackburn and Blackpool, under the TechFirst programme, with a stated intention to roll out an England-wide AI skills and early careers scheme next summer subject to pilot results. The relevance is the cohort and the clock. This is the nearest-term national AI skills vehicle aimed at the 18 to 24 group where the borough is most exposed, the North East is not in the pilot, and a summer 2027 national launch sits at the far edge of the regional window identified in the NEET work. There is no funding figure, prospectus or named geography for the roll-out yet, so this is a thing to position for rather than bid into.

Mon 17 Aug

Housing density around stations

Why it matters: a default position in favour of housing within walking distance of well-connected stations, minimum densities around them, reduced statutory consultee requirements and 40 per cent accessible homes on major developments. The North East is not named, but on the face of it the Metro and rail corridors through Gateshead Interchange, Felling, Pelaw, Dunston, Blaydon and the MetroCentre would fall within scope. An early spatial test of which centres would qualify is cheap to do and connects directly to the centres work. National planning policy and secondary legislation detail is still to follow, so this is an announcement rather than a rule.

Our own record, corrected

Regional gross disposable household income is confirmed for Wednesday. Last Monday this edition flagged that GDHI 1997 to 2024 had lost its date, the release page having changed from 19 August to "provisional release date: August 2026". That has reversed. The page now reads "Release date: 19 August 2026 9:30am", the calendar records it as finalised, and it covers ITL regions together with local and combined authorities. It takes Gateshead's GDHI per head from 2023 to 2024. The 20 August monthly brief slot therefore stands as planned, with the new figure one day old. One caveat carries over — this is the local and combined authority edition, and the separate small-area GDHI release is not on the calendar to December, so the lower geography does not refresh with it.
The productivity method paper is September, not November. The last two editions timed the move to a component-based method for the headline productivity statistic at November, alongside the 2024 subregional productivity release. The methods and impacts article is now calendared for 28 September, with the release page giving "September to October 2026". That is the better outcome — the method lands roughly five weeks before the numbers, so the back series can be read against an explained method rather than at the same moment. Subregional productivity for 2024 remains confirmed for 5 November, having been postponed from 27 August for geography updates and revisions to deflation methodology, and remains badged official statistics in development.
UK Business Counts is September, not the autumn. The 2026 edition, published as "UK business; activity, size and location", is confirmed for 24 September. Our internal dataset notes place it in October or November, which is wrong for this year and worth correcting before anything is planned around it. By contrast, regional GDP for 1998 to 2024 is not confirmed for 23 September — that date is the calendar's placeholder and the release page commits only to September 2026, so downstream work should not be pinned to it yet.

Filtered out this week

Parliament has been in summer recess since 16 July and does not return until 1 September, so there were no written statements, committee sessions or bill stages in the window. On fiscal devolution specifically there was no movement at all — nothing on the equalisation design, nothing on which grants are abolished in the income tax swap, and no trail of the roadmap. That silence is worth recording, because it is the question the team most needs a position on before 28 October, and the answer is still six weeks further away than the announcement implied. Also set aside — a correction to an analytical annex table in the young people and work interim report, checked against our own outputs and cited in none of them; the 13 August national accounts releases, all UK level; local authority revenue and capital outturn statistics, which are council finance rather than economic intelligence; the precarious work and economically inactive survey technical report, with no local breakdown; the national survey of exporting behaviours; updated subsidy control guidance; the algorithmic transparency records finder; the Timms review co-chair update; T Level and A Level results-day figures; an automotive innovation funding programme uplift; and the usual Companies House, research council and financial regulator notices. All tracked, none actioned.

Two quiet weeks, and neither of them empty. No local statistic has been published since 3 August, and the drought ends tomorrow with the July claimant count and on Wednesday with a new GDHI year. The value in this edition sits elsewhere. The status of the survey behind every borough labour market figure was settled on 11 August, and settled downwards in a way that has to be reflected in how those figures are labelled rather than argued with. A reduced sample boost is coming, and September will tell us what it costs at local authority level. Against that, the high streets package is the first national measure in a while that acts on something we can actually see in the priority centres — and the work it calls for is a baseline count taken before the rules change, not after.
