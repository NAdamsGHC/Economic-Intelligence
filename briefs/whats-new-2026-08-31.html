What's New · weekly round-up · covering 25 – 31 August

What's New, 31 August 2026

A short weekly read of what's coming, what's landed and what matters for Gateshead's economy. Each item says why it's here. This edition is back on schedule and covers the seven days since the last one. No new economic statistic for the borough landed in the window, and the next one is a fortnight away. What did land was the evidence base behind the government's review of young people and work, published in a single drop on 27 August, on the same morning as the national NEET figures.

The youth employment evidence base opened up this week, and the policy window closes in a matter of weeks. On 27 August the Department for Work and Pensions published the six research reports underpinning the Young people and work review, which had been running since May on a diagnosis with no published evidence behind it. The same morning the ONS published the national NEET estimates for April to June 2026. On 2 September, in two days, the ONS publishes Census-based NEET estimates for England at sub-national level, which is the nearest thing yet to the measurement gap our own NEET work identified. The review's solutions report is due in early autumn 2026. That sequence puts every piece of evidence we would need to make a Gateshead case on the table within a fortnight, and puts it there before the report that decides what happens next is written.

The Local Numbers

Thu 27 Aug · new local dataset

Gateshead now has a population series by single year of age out to 2050, and half of it must not be used as a forecast

Why it matters: the ONS has published mid-year population estimates and re-scaled population estimates for local authorities in England by sex and single year of age, 2025 to 2050 (ONS, ad hoc release 3596, published 27 August 2026). The 2025 column is the mid-2025 estimate, which is the base our own per-head denominators have been lagging behind since it was published on 29 July. Every rate we publish on a working-age denominator (the claimant count proportion above all) is still being computed by NOMIS on the superseded mid-2024 base, and this release gives us the current one at single year of age, so we can state the size of the difference rather than caveat it.

The years after 2025 are a different object and need labelling as one. The 2026 to 2050 columns are not local projections. The ONS applies the national single year of age and sex change from the national population projections across all English local authorities using a scaling factor. Gateshead's future in this dataset is therefore England's future rescaled to Gateshead's 2025 starting point, and it carries no local assumption about births, deaths or migration at all. It is a useful bridge between the latest estimates and the national projections, which is what the ONS says it is for. It is not a Gateshead population forecast, and it would be wrong in a direction we could predict if it were used as one, because the borough's migration and age structure do not track the national pattern. The 2022-based subnational projections remain the local projection of record.

No new economic statistic for the borough landed this week. The claimant count for July 2026 was published on 18 August, and the next Gateshead figure of any kind comes with the labour market release on 15 September, which is also the quarterly refresh of the PAYE local authority sheet.

The Centre of Government

Mon 24 Aug · MHCLG · tier of government

The consultation on what planning powers mayors actually get is open, and it closes on 5 October

Why it matters: the government is consulting on how the planning powers in the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 should be implemented for mayors of strategic authorities (MHCLG, Planning powers for mayors in England, opened 24 August 2026, closes 11.59pm on 5 October 2026). It covers mayoral call-in of planning applications of potential strategic importance, Mayoral Development Orders, and Community Infrastructure Levy arrangements. This is the first of the post-Act consultations to reach the machinery that decides who determines a large application in this borough.

This is a boundary question before it is a planning question. The 31 July Cabinet Statement set the direction of travel on what strategic authorities hold, and the white paper that settles it does not arrive until the Budget on 28 October. This consultation closes three weeks before that. A power to call in applications of strategic importance moves a decision from the borough to the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority, and the definition of strategic importance is what determines how often that happens. The consultation does not name the North East or any individual authority, so the thresholds in it apply uniformly and their local effect depends entirely on the borough's own pipeline of large sites. Working out how many Gateshead applications over the last five years would have met the proposed thresholds is a tractable piece of analysis, and the only way to answer the question with a number rather than a view.

Mon 24 & Tue 25 Aug · MHCLG · devolved funding

The North East gets £445 million and around 3,400 homes in the first wave of the Social and Affordable Homes Programme

Why it matters: the government has confirmed initial allocations from the £39 billion Social and Affordable Homes Programme, a first wave of £9.58 billion supporting more than 70,000 homes across England (MHCLG, 25 August 2026, and the accompanying policy document The Social and Affordable Homes Programme and the reinvigoration of council housebuilding). The North East is named as an established Mayoral Strategic Authority with an estimated spend of £445 million supporting approximately 3,400 homes. For comparison, Greater Manchester has £529 million and around 4,400 homes, West Yorkshire £441 million and around 4,000, the West Midlands £409 million and around 3,200, Liverpool City Region £380 million and around 3,100, and South Yorkshire £249 million and around 2,200. Around 60 per cent of homes delivered through these partnerships are expected to be for social rent.

No borough-level split has been published, and that is the number that matters here. The allocation is made to the strategic authority rather than to the seven councils, so how much of the £445 million reaches Gateshead is a question for the combined authority's own programme and not for this release. What can be said now is the context. The borough passed the 2025 Housing Delivery Test at 105 per cent on the strength of a single strong year (546 net additions in 2024/25 against 349 the year before), which is a pass with very little margin in it, and the programme's stated emphasis on council housebuilding is the first funding stream in years aimed squarely at the tenure that would widen that margin. The related programme supporting council capacity to build has been renamed Capacity to Build and extended to cover 2026 to 2029.

Policy and National Data

Thu 27 Aug · DWP · evidence base

Six research reports behind the young people and work review, and one of them tells us what employers actually think

Why it matters: DWP published the full research base underpinning the independent Young people and work review on 27 August, as six ad hoc research reports. They are a survey of young people who are NEET, qualitative research with NEET 16 to 24 year olds not claiming health or disability benefits, a survey of healthcare professionals, a survey of education professionals, an employer views survey, and general population polling (DWP ad hoc research). The review's interim report was published on 28 May 2026 and its solutions report is due in early autumn 2026. Until this week the interim diagnosis was public and the evidence underneath it was not.

The employer survey is the one with usable numbers, and it points at supply of experience rather than supply of workers. It is 500 telephone interviews with employers, fielded 4 to 18 March 2026 (Employer views on hiring young people survey report). Of those employers, 42 per cent had hired 16 to 24 year olds. The most common difficulties reported in hiring young people were a lack of soft skills and work readiness (41 per cent), a lack of work experience (40 per cent) and concerns about resilience (32 per cent). Sixty-two per cent felt young people were less ready to excel at work than other generations, and 32 per cent said they were less likely to hire someone under 25 than an older worker, although 50 per cent reported about the same likelihood for both. On the education system, 91 per cent agreed there should be more vocational pathways, 89 per cent supported careers guidance in schools and colleges, and 76 per cent felt the current curriculum does not prepare students adequately for work. Fifty-five per cent had engaged with an education or employment support provider, most often a college (38 per cent), and the most common barrier to doing so was finding the time for staff to manage it (41 per cent).

These are national estimates from a sample of 500 and they carry sampling uncertainty, which the report should be read for before any figure is quoted onward. No regional breakdown is published beyond a single England comparison, so none of this can be stated as a Gateshead finding. What it does is change what a Gateshead submission would have to argue. Our own NEET work found the borough strong at 16 to 17 and exposed at 18 to 24, which is the point at which the education system stops holding people and the labour market has to. The employer evidence suggests that what employers are short of at that handover is work experience and the confidence that comes with it, and that half of those who do not engage with providers cite staff time rather than unwillingness. Both of those are addressable locally, and neither requires a change in the benefit system.

Thu 27 Aug · ONS · national data

981,000 young people are NEET across the UK, and nine tenths of them are over 18

Why it matters: the estimated number of young people aged 16 to 24 in the UK who were NEET in April to June 2026 is 981,000, which is 13.0 per cent of all people in that age group (ONS, Young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), UK: August 2026, released 27 August 2026). That is 30,000 more than a year earlier and 0.2 percentage points up on the year, and 30,000 fewer than the previous quarter and 0.5 percentage points down on the quarter. Of the total, 894,000 are aged 18 to 24, a rate of 15.2 per cent, up 17,000 on the year and down 33,000 on the quarter. By sex, 530,000 young men (13.7 per cent) and 451,000 young women (12.3 per cent).

The age split is the finding, and it is the same split we found locally. Of the 981,000, some 894,000 are aged 18 to 24, which is around 91 per cent of the UK's NEET young people. The ONS advises caution on short-term changes in this series because of sampling variability, and directs users to long-term movements, so the quarterly fall should not be read as an improvement and the annual rise should not be read as a deterioration without testing. These are Labour Force Survey estimates and they are not published for local authorities, which is exactly the gap the 2 September Census-based release starts to fill.

Fri 28 Aug · ONS · national data

For the first time since March 2025, low-income households face lower inflation than high-income ones

Why it matters: the Household Costs Index for all UK households rose 2.8 per cent in the year to June 2026, down from 3.6 per cent in March 2026 (ONS, Household Costs Indices for UK household groups: April to June 2026, released 28 August 2026). Low-income households (the second income decile) saw 2.7 per cent and high-income households (the ninth decile) 2.8 per cent, the first time since March 2025 that lower-income households have experienced lower inflation than higher-income households. Retired households saw 2.5 per cent against 2.9 per cent for non-retired, a gap of 0.4 percentage points and the widest since April 2025. The ONS attributes the reversal at the bottom of the distribution to falls in the energy price cap, because electricity, gas and other fuels take a larger share of spending in low-income households.

This is the closest published counterpart to the cost-of-living index built here, and it is a national series. Gateshead's income distribution sits well below the UK's, so the second decile is a better proxy for the typical local household than the all-household figure is, and on that basis the squeeze eased slightly faster here than the headline suggests. The mechanism matters for the forward read: this improvement is an energy price effect, so it reverses if the cap rises, and it says nothing about any change in the underlying position. Nothing in this release is published below UK level.

Wed 26 & Fri 28 Aug · DWP

The Jobs Guarantee has started paying wages

Why it matters: the first young people supported by the Jobs Guarantee have started work, announced on 26 August for the programme overall and on 28 August for the first starts in Wales (gov.uk, 26 August 2026). The programme targets the long-term unemployed. This is the first delivery evidence from a national youth employment programme since the two packages that went live at the end of June, and it is the sort of thing that produces evaluable outcomes rather than announcements. No participant numbers, no cost per start and no regional breakdown have been published, so there is nothing here to benchmark against yet.

Watch it because employment support is named for devolution. The 31 July Cabinet Statement committed to devolving employment support to mayors, including the power to procure and provide tailored support for the long-term unemployed. The Jobs Guarantee is the current centrally run version of exactly that. How it is delivered, and what it costs per outcome, is the baseline any mayoral alternative would be judged against.

Tue 25 Aug · DWP

A second English council area gets a practical AI-for-jobseekers scheme

Why it matters: two new schemes are launching in Barnsley to help residents use AI in practical ways, from applying for jobs to preparing for interviews (gov.uk, 25 August 2026). Barnsley is a comparable place, a former industrial borough inside a mayoral strategic authority with a labour market profile close to ours. The relevance is direct. Our own reading of the youth employment position identified a window for a North East AI-and-work pilot before early 2027, and this is the second place to get one first. Each of these makes the case easier to argue from precedent and harder to argue as novel. No evaluation, cost or participant numbers have been published.

Tue 25 & Fri 28 Aug · DBIST

Electronic balloting is now law, and the zero hours guidance has landed without commencement dates

Why it matters: electronic and workplace balloting for statutory trade union ballots came into force on 25 August 2026, confirmed in the updated implementation timeline for the Plan to Make Work Pay and the Employment Rights Act 2025 (gov.uk, 25 August 2026), with the supporting code of practice linked from the three affected trade union guidance pages the same morning. This was carried as a forthcoming item in the 10 August edition and is now closed.

The zero hours guidance is the one to watch, and it is deliberately empty. New guidance for employers on zero hours contracts was first published on 28 August (gov.uk, 28 August 2026). It sets out the law as it stands and states that the new zero hours measures are not yet in force and will change once the Employment Rights Act 2025 is fully implemented. No commencement date is given for guaranteed hours or for reasonable notice of shifts. The consultation on ending one-sided flexibility closed on 25 August, three days before this appeared, and we recorded at the time that we made no response because we hold no reliable local measure of zero-hours prevalence. That remains the honest position and it remains a gap. When these rights do commence, we will have no baseline against which to say what changed in this borough.

Tue 25 Aug · DfE

£25 million for care leavers, alongside a review of early deaths among care-experienced young adults

Why it matters: DfE announced £25 million to help care leavers access homes with support, published alongside the report of the independent review of deaths among care-experienced young adults in England (gov.uk, 25 August 2026, and Review of early deaths of care leavers). Care leavers are one of the groups most heavily over-represented in the 18 to 24 NEET population, and this council is their corporate parent, so it is one of the few cohorts where the local authority holds both the duty and the data. This is not primarily an economic item and should not be written up as one. It belongs in the same evidence picture as the young people and work reports above, and the two landed within 48 hours of each other.

Mon 24 & Thu 27 Aug · DWP · statistics

Two changes to the DWP statistical estate that affect what we can cut locally

Why it matters: DWP published its response to the consultation on ceasing to report the Parental Conflict Indicator as an official statistic, and that consultation is now concluded (gov.uk, 24 August 2026). Separately, the DWP statistical work programme was updated on 27 August to reflect changes to administrative geography information, the status of the Parental Conflict Indicator, and confirmed statistical developments (DWP statistical work programme). The administrative geography changes are the ones with teeth for us. Every DWP local cut we take through Stat-Xplore depends on the geography lookup being stable, and a change there is a silent break rather than a visible one. The work programme should be read in full before the next benefits cut is taken, not after.

Also this week, the benefit cap statistics. DWP announced that outcomes for off-flows, previously withdrawn, will be reinstated (Benefit Cap statistics). Benefit cap counts are available for Gateshead through Stat-Xplore, so this restores a measure we can take locally.

A quiet week for the borough's own numbers and a loud one for the evidence behind them. Nothing new was published about Gateshead's economy between 25 and 31 August, and nothing will be until 15 September. What did happen is that three things converged on the same question. The evidence base behind the government's review of young people and work went public on 27 August, the national NEET estimates were published the same morning, and the first sub-national NEET measure lands on 2 September. The review's recommendations follow in early autumn. Our own reading of this borough's position (strong at 16 to 17, exposed at 18 to 24) now has a national evidence base that points at the same handover, and an employer survey that says the shortage at that point is work experience rather than willingness to hire. Alongside it, the machinery moved: £445 million of social housing money is now sitting with the strategic authority rather than with the seven councils, and the consultation deciding which planning decisions move up to the mayor closes on 5 October, three weeks before the white paper that settles the rest. The centre published nothing at all on the fiscal mechanism for a fifth week. Next edition: Monday 7 September.
