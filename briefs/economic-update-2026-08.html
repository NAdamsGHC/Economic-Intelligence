Monthly Economic Update — August 2026

Nathaniel Adams, Economic Intelligence Analyst · published 25 August 2026

The monthly update between quarterly editions. It was due on 20 August, two working days after the labour market release, and is published late; it therefore covers everything since the July quarterly update of 3 August rather than a shorter slice, so nothing falls between the two. The Quarterly Economic Update dashboard carries the full series behind everything below. Every figure here was pulled fresh from the official source on 25 August 2026.

The month in three lines

What changed. Two things, and they point in opposite directions. Gateshead's claimant count for July 2026 is 5,035 people, a rate of 4.0 per cent of residents aged 16 to 64 — 45 higher than June, but only 10 higher than July 2025. That is flat on the year, at plus 0.2 per cent, while the North East rose 2.0 per cent and England 1.1 per cent. Against that, a new household income year landed on 19 August, and it shows Gateshead's income per head falling further behind the country for the third year running.

What it means. The borough has come off the regional path. In the year to July 2025 Gateshead's claimant count fell 12.5 per cent, almost exactly in step with the North East at 12.8 per cent and much faster than England at 5.5 per cent. In the year to July 2026 the region has turned and risen 2.0 per cent while Gateshead has stayed flat — so the borough is no longer moving with its neighbours, and for once that is the better side of the divergence. The household income measure is not a labour market measure, though, and it says something the claimant count cannot: income per head grew 5.8 per cent in 2024 and the gap to the UK still widened, because the UK grew 6.2 per cent. Standing still on unemployment is compatible with falling behind on income, and in 2024 both were true at once.

What to watch. Young people. The claimant count for those aged 18 to 24 in Gateshead is 990 in July 2026, up 8.8 per cent since June 2025, against 7.7 per cent for the North East and 10.1 per cent for England. It is a rise on a base of under a thousand, so it moves easily and no ranking should be read into the gaps, but it is the one part of the local picture moving the wrong way while the total holds. The next reading is 15 September, which also brings the quarterly refresh of the payrolled employee figure and the ONS quality article on the reduced Annual Population Survey sample.

Households: a new income year, and a wider gap

Gateshead's gross disposable household income per head of population at current basic prices for 2024 is £19,745, up 5.8 per cent on the £18,657 recorded for 2023 (ONS, Regional gross disposable household income (GDHI), UK: 1997 to 2024, released 19 August 2026). The UK figure is £25,965 and the North East figure is £20,562, both as published. Gateshead sits below its own region as well as below the UK, and ranks 338th of the 361 UK local authorities in the dataset.

The cash figure is not the finding. Measured as an index against the UK at 100, Gateshead was 76.3 in 2023 and is 76.0 in 2024 — the lowest reading since 2008, and the third consecutive annual fall from the 2020 peak of 77.9. Income per head is rising in cash terms and slipping relative to the country at every step, because the UK is growing faster.

Where the money comes from matters for what can be done about it. Per head, compensation of employees — the wage and salary component — is £16,448 in 2024, up 4.9 per cent on the year, and it is both the largest component and one of the slowest growing. Social benefits received are £7,135 per head, up 7.0 per cent. Property income paid, which is dominated by mortgage interest, is £990 per head against £222 in 2019, a more than fourfold rise over five years that comes straight off disposable income. In short, the growth in 2024 came disproportionately from transfers and asset income rather than from earnings, and a rising slice of it went back out again as interest.

Two cautions on this release. First, the local authority figures are in current prices only — nothing here is adjusted for inflation, so the 5.8 per cent is nominal and must be labelled as such. Second, real household disposable income is published only at regional level, and there the ONS reports that "the weakest growth in RHDI per head was seen in the North East of England at 2.2%", against 3.1 per cent for the UK and the lowest of the twelve ITL1 regions. That regional figure is the closest available read on what the borough's households actually experienced, and it should be quoted as regional, not local.

The claimant count, and where it sits

Gateshead's claimant count for July 2026 is 5,035 people, a rate of 4.0 per cent of residents aged 16 to 64 (NOMIS, Claimant count, dataset NM_162_1, July 2026). On the level, that puts the borough above Northumberland (2.8 per cent), County Durham (3.1), North Tyneside (3.3) and Sunderland (3.8), level with Newcastle upon Tyne (4.0) and England (4.0), and below South Tyneside (4.7). On the trend, the position is better than the level suggests: over the year Gateshead is up 0.2 per cent against 2.0 per cent for the North East and 1.1 per cent for England, and only Sunderland, which fell 1.7 per cent, did better among the seven North East Mayoral Strategic Authority councils.

Local authority claimant counts are published without seasonal adjustment, so the month-on-month rise of 45 should not be read on its own; the year-on-year comparison is the one that controls for the time of year, and it is flat. The claimant count is an administrative count with no sampling error, but it is classed experimental across the Universal Credit transition, so trend is the story and level comparisons across the transition are not.

Ward detail, July 2026

The borough's spread is wider than any comparison with a neighbouring council. Felling, at 6.6 per cent, has more than four and a half times the claimant rate of Low Fell, at 1.4 per cent. Reading the table. Survey estimates carry a 95 per cent confidence interval, shown for Gateshead; administrative and national accounts series do not have one and the column reads n/a rather than being left to look like a zero. None of the four Annual Population Survey measures has a new quarter this month — the survey refreshes with the September dataset — so they are shown at their true period, Apr 2025 to Mar 2026, and marked as carrying no new data rather than restated as though they were current. Note that Gateshead's employment rate confidence interval of plus or minus 4.2 percentage points is wider than the entire gap to England, so no comparison drawn from those rows is statistically supportable.

The national picture behind it

The UK employment rate for those aged 16 to 64 for April to June 2026 is 75.1 per cent, the unemployment rate for those aged 16 and over is 4.9 per cent, and the economic inactivity rate for those aged 16 to 64 is 20.9 per cent (ONS, Labour market overview, UK: August 2026). Payrolled employees fell by 78,000, or 0.3 per cent, between June 2025 and June 2026. Vacancies for May to July 2026 fell to 707,000, the lowest level in more than five years, with the fall attributed mainly to smaller businesses citing labour and operating costs as reasons for not hiring or replacing leavers. Annual growth in regular pay was 3.5 per cent, which is 0.5 per cent in real terms on the CPIH measure.

Inflation turned back up in the same window. CPI rose by 2.9 per cent in the 12 months to July 2026, from 2.6 per cent in June, and CPIH by 3.1 per cent from 2.8 per cent (ONS, Consumer price inflation, UK: July 2026), driven by housing and household services following the July energy price cap increase and partly offset by falling motor fuel prices. An energy-led reacceleration is regressive by construction, and it lands on households whose real income growth is already the weakest in the country.

Two points connect the national picture to the local one. Vacancies are being squeezed at the small-employer end, which is where a disproportionate share of the borough's private sector jobs sit. And real pay growth of half a per cent means that the wage component of household income — the largest and the slowest-growing component in the 2024 accounts — is unlikely to be the thing that closes the gap in the next reading either.
