What's New · weekly round-up · covering 17 – 25 August

What's New — 25 August 2026

A short weekly read of what's coming, what's landed and what matters for Gateshead's economy. Each item says why it's here. This is the Monday 24 August edition, published a day late, and it covers the full window since the last edition on 17 August rather than a seven-day slice — nine days, so nothing falls between the two. It was a heavy nine days. The statistical drought broke with three genuinely local numbers, England's planning rulebook was replaced, and a government department changed its name without our noticing.

England has a new National Planning Policy Framework, and it reached us five days late. On 17 August the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government published a revised National Planning Policy Framework, the government response to the reform consultation, the government response on the statutory consultee system, a policy statement titled Creating a clear, rules-based planning system, and both the 2024 and the 2025 Housing Delivery Test measurements — one package, one day. None of it arrived here until the weekly departmental email on Saturday 22 August, because the framework is a guidance page rather than a news item. The framework itself runs to 130 pages and the consultation response to 155, and neither has been read here yet. That read is the largest single piece of work this edition generates, and it should not be done from the press notice.

Landed — the local numbers

Wed 19 Aug · new local figure

Household incomes: Gateshead's GDHI per head reaches £19,745, and the gap to the UK widens again

Why it matters: Gateshead's gross disposable household income per head of population at current basic prices for 2024 is £19,745, up 5.8 per cent on the £18,657 recorded for 2023 (ONS, Regional gross disposable household income (GDHI), UK: 1997 to 2024, released 19 August 2026). It is the first new GDHI year for the borough since the 1997 to 2023 edition. Against it, the UK figure is £25,965 and the North East figure is £20,562, both as published. Gateshead therefore sits below its own region as well as below the UK, and ranks 338th of the 361 UK local authorities in the dataset.

The number that actually moved is the index. Measured against the UK at 100, Gateshead was 77.5 in 2019, 76.3 in 2023 and 76.0 in 2024. Income per head is rising in cash terms and slipping relative to the country at every one of those steps. The direction is the finding, not the level: a borough can grow at 5.8 per cent and still lose ground, and this one did, because the UK grew at 6.2 per cent. Nothing in the release is adjusted for inflation at local authority level, so this is a nominal series and has to be labelled as one.

In real terms the region went backwards relative to everywhere else. The ONS reports that real household disposable income per head in the UK rose 3.1 per cent in 2024, and that "the weakest growth in RHDI per head was seen in the North East of England at 2.2%" — the lowest of the twelve ITL1 regions. Real household income is not published at local authority level, so this regional figure is the closest available read on what the borough's households actually experienced, and it should be quoted as regional rather than local.

Mon 17 Aug · new local figure

Housing Delivery Test: Gateshead moves from 93 per cent and an action plan to 105 per cent and no consequence

Why it matters: both the 2024 and the 2025 Housing Delivery Test measurements were published on the same day. On the 2024 measurement, covering 2021/22 to 2023/24, Gateshead delivered 1,203 homes against a requirement of 1,292 — 93 per cent, carrying an action plan consequence. On the 2025 measurement, covering 2022/23 to 2024/25, the borough delivered 1,340 against a requirement of 1,271 — 105 per cent, no consequence. The 2025 measurement supersedes the 2024 one for decision-making. The swing is almost entirely one year: 546 net additions in 2024/25 against 349 the year before.

Read it against the neighbours, because the region moved both ways at once. On the 2025 measurement Northumberland is at 262 per cent, Sunderland 167, County Durham 131 and Gateshead 105, all with no consequence. Newcastle upon Tyne falls from 102 per cent to 87 and picks up an action plan. North Tyneside (65 per cent) and South Tyneside (53 per cent) stay under the presumption in favour of sustainable development. Four of the seven North East Mayoral Strategic Authority councils clear the bar and three do not, which makes a combined-authority housing narrative harder to write than a borough one. The measurement is a three-year rolling ratio, so a single strong year can carry it and a single weak year can lose it — 105 per cent is a pass with very little margin in it.

Tue 18 Aug · new local figure

July claimant count: Gateshead flat on the year while the region and England rise

Why it matters: Gateshead's claimant count for July 2026 is 5,035 people, a rate of 4.0 per cent of residents aged 16 to 64 (NOMIS, Claimant count, dataset NM_162_1, July 2026). That is 45 more than June and 10 more than July 2025 — flat on the year at plus 0.2 per cent, against plus 2.0 per cent across the North East and plus 1.1 per cent across England. On the level rather than the trend, Gateshead's 4.0 per cent sits above Northumberland (2.8), County Durham (3.1), North Tyneside (3.3) and Sunderland (3.8), level with Newcastle upon Tyne (4.0) and England (4.0), and below South Tyneside (4.7).

The exception is young people, and it is the same exception as last month. The claimant count for those aged 18 to 24 in Gateshead is 990 in July 2026, up 8.8 per cent on June 2025 — a rise on a base of under a thousand, so it moves easily, but pointing the same way as the region (up 7.7 per cent) and England (up 10.1 per cent). The claimant count is an administrative count with no sampling confidence interval, and it is classed experimental across the Universal Credit transition, so trend is the story and level comparisons across the transition are not. August is not a quarterly refresh month for the PAYE local authority sheet, so there is no new payrolled employee figure for the borough until 15 September.

Landed — the centre of government

Fri 21 Aug · machinery of government

The Department for Business and Trade no longer exists under that name

Why it matters: the Intellectual Property Office announced a new minister responsible for intellectual property "based within the newly formed Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade" (gov.uk, 21 August 2026). The department's own gov.uk organisation page now carries the banner "This organisation is changing. It's now called the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade." The trade remedies notices published in the same window are issued by "the Secretary of State for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade". The reorganisation dates from July 2026.

This is a machinery-of-government change and it has a direct operational consequence. A department that sponsors business support, trade, industrial strategy and the Make Work Pay programme has absorbed innovation and science, which is a different centre of gravity for the industrial strategy work and for anything routed through the sector plans. It also means our subscribed gov.uk alert is registered against an organisation that is being renamed underneath it: the daily feed still arrives headed "Department for Business and Trade", and if the old organisation page is eventually retired the feed goes quiet without an error. That needs re-pointing rather than watching. It is worth recording plainly that the change happened in July and reached this weekly only in late August, through an agency newsletter rather than through any of the four subscribed departmental feeds.

Fri 31 Jul · Cabinet Office · reported here now

Rewiring the State — the half of it we did not report

Why it matters: the fiscal half of the 31 July Cabinet Statement — a share of business rates retained by mayors from April 2027 and a share of local income tax replacing central grant from 2028 — has been covered here and in the August policy update. The rest of the statement has not, and it acts on the machinery this borough's evidence base is built on. Every quotation below has been checked verbatim against the published statement (Cabinet Office, Rewiring the State, 31 July 2026).

Strategic authorities everywhere. The stated ambition is that "every area in England has, or is in the process of establishing, a strategic authority by the end of 2027, with strategic authorities in place everywhere by the end of 2028". This part is not about us — the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority already exists and has a mayor. What matters here is not whether a strategic authority exists but what one holds, which is the subject of the autumn white paper.

Public service geography is to be redrawn to fit. The statement commits to "new local police units aligning with local authorities, and regional police forces, fire and rescue services and Integrated Care Boards aligning with the boundaries of strategic authorities by the end of the Parliament". None of those three currently fits the strategic authority boundary. Northumbria Police covers six of the seven North East Mayoral Strategic Authority councils, with County Durham policed by Durham Constabulary, which also covers Darlington in the Tees Valley. Fire and rescue is split three ways over the same seven councils — Tyne and Wear Fire and Rescue Service covers Gateshead, Newcastle, North Tyneside, South Tyneside and Sunderland; Northumberland Fire and Rescue Service covers Northumberland; and County Durham and Darlington Fire and Rescue Service covers County Durham together with Darlington, again crossing the boundary. The North East and North Cumbria Integrated Care Board states that it looks after 3.1 million people; the seven councils had a combined population of 2.05 million at mid-2025, so roughly a third of the board's population sits outside the boundary it is to be aligned to.

The consequence here is measurement before it is anything else. Health-related economic inactivity is the clearest example: it is the driver we can currently only assert at regional level, and the commissioning geography that produces the evidence for it is the Integrated Care Board footprint. If that footprint moves, the series moves with it, and the break falls in the middle of the period the Local Economic Assessment covers. The same applies to any analysis built on police force area or fire authority geography.

The burden of proof reverses. The statement adopts "an operating principle of devolving by default – meaning that where policies or services are being designed or altered, Secretaries of State will need to justify holding them at the level of central government", and states that "Central government must become smaller and more strategic, releasing savings and enabling resources to be transferred to local areas alongside policy responsibilities". Read plainly, that changes what an evidence ask has to prove: the question moves from whether a function should come down to whether there is a reason for it to stay up.

Employment support is named for devolution. The statement commits to "devolve employment support to mayors to ensure provision reflects local need, including giving local areas the power to procure and provide tailored and specialised support for the long-term unemployed". This is the commitment closest to work already live here. The borough's exposure is concentrated in the 18 to 24 group, and the practical route into a mayoral power is through the strategic authority rather than directly. No design, funding or timetable has been published, so this is a position to be ready for rather than a programme to plan against.

The next dated moment is September. The statement says that "in September, we will meet with all the mayors, as well as our permanent secretaries, the Cabinet Secretary and the mayors' chief executives, to discuss progress on rewiring the state". No date has been announced and nothing has been published from it as at today. After that, the white paper and the fiscal devolution roadmap land with the Budget on 28 October.

Tue 18 Aug · Prime Minister

Free bus travel extended to all hours for disabled people

Why it matters: the Prime Minister announced that time restrictions on concessionary passes will be lifted, so eligible disabled people in England get free bus travel all day, every day (gov.uk, 18 August 2026). It carries no local statistic and it is not an indicator change, but it is a Prime Minister's announcement and so it is named here rather than folded away. The read is narrow and worth stating rather than inflating: concessionary travel reimbursement is a transport authority cost, the measure removes a time barrier for a group whose labour market participation is a live question here, and it lands in the same window as the health-related items in the departmental log below. No cost figure or commencement date is given in the announcement.

Landed — policy and national data

Mon 17 Aug, alerted Sat 22 Aug

The planning package: new Framework, two consultation responses, and a rules-based policy statement

Why it matters: the revised National Planning Policy Framework replaces the December 2024 version and is described on its own page as having been "revised in response to the National Planning Policy Framework: proposed reforms and other changes to the planning system consultation". Alongside it came the government response to that consultation with a Public Sector Equality Duty assessment, the government response on reforms to the statutory consultee system, the policy statement Creating a clear, rules-based planning system, and an updated Housing Delivery Test measurement rule book. England-only.

What is honest to say today, and what is not. Honest: the framework has been replaced, the two consultations are closed and answered, and the Housing Delivery Test results published the same day are the first measured under the surrounding changes. Not honest: any summary of what the new Framework actually changes for Gateshead, because 285 pages of Framework and response have not been read. This edition is not going to paraphrase a press notice and call it a read. The station-density measures trailed in the 17 August edition are the visible thread — "Full steam ahead to fast-track more homes near stations" was published in the same package — and the Metro and rail corridors through Gateshead Interchange, Felling, Pelaw, Dunston, Blaydon and the MetroCentre would be in scope on the face of it. That remains an announcement-level read until the Framework text is checked.

Tue 18 Aug

UK labour market, August 2026 — steady rates, softer demand underneath

Why it matters: the UK employment rate for those aged 16 to 64 for April to June 2026 is 75.1 per cent, the unemployment rate for those aged 16 and over is 4.9 per cent, and the economic inactivity rate for those aged 16 to 64 is 20.9 per cent (ONS, Labour market overview, UK: August 2026). Payrolled employees fell by 78,000, or 0.3 per cent, between June 2025 and June 2026. Vacancies for May to July 2026 fell to 707,000, down 6,000 on the quarter and the lowest in more than five years, with the ONS attributing the fall mainly to smaller businesses citing labour and operating costs. Annual growth in regular pay was 3.5 per cent, or 0.5 per cent in real terms on the CPIH measure.

The local read is the one above, not this one. These are UK figures; the Annual Population Survey estimates that would give the borough equivalents do not refresh this month. The pattern to carry forward is that vacancies are being squeezed at the small-employer end, which is where a disproportionate share of the borough's private sector jobs sit, and that real pay growth has almost stopped at half a per cent.

Wed 19 Aug

Inflation back up: CPI at 2.9 per cent, CPIH at 3.1 per cent

Why it matters: CPI rose by 2.9 per cent in the 12 months to July 2026, up from 2.6 per cent in June; CPIH rose by 3.1 per cent, up from 2.8 per cent (ONS, Consumer price inflation, UK: July 2026). The ONS records that "Housing and household services, and furniture made the largest upward contributions to the monthly change in both CPIH and CPI annual rates; transport made the largest, partially offsetting, downward contribution", with the housing contribution driven by the July energy price cap increase and the transport offset by falling motor fuel prices.

Why this one matters more here than the headline suggests. An energy-led reacceleration is regressive by construction, and the cost-of-living index built here weights household energy heavily for exactly that reason. It also sets the deflator context for the GDHI item above: 2024's 5.8 per cent nominal growth in household income was earned in a lower-inflation year than the one now running, so the read-across from nominal 2024 income to household experience in 2026 is not direct.

Thu 20 Aug

Blue Book 2026 impact articles — GDP is about to be revised under us

Why it matters: the ONS published its Blue Book 2026 impact articles setting out how this year's methods and sources changes affect estimates of the size and growth of the UK economy, covering GDP impacts and expenditure components and an industry impact analysis. The improvements enter headline GDP from September, with revisions taking in data up to Quarter 2 2026, and a user webinar is set for 30 September alongside the latest Quarterly National Accounts.

The reason to watch it is downstream. Regional and subregional GVA are built consistently with the national accounts, so a Blue Book change propagates into the regional accounts at the next annual run — which is the 2024 GVA year expected in September and subregional productivity for 2024 confirmed for 5 November. Any back-series movement in Gateshead's GVA between now and November needs attributing to method rather than to the borough's economy, and the impact articles are the document that lets that attribution be made rather than guessed.

Wed 19 Aug & Fri 21 Aug

DWP statistics: Universal Credit to May 2026, sanctions to May 2026, and the Jobs Guarantee

Why it matters: two DWP statistical releases in the window carry local authority detail through Stat-Xplore rather than through a published table — Universal Credit quarterly statistics to 14 May 2026 (claims, starts, people and households) and Benefit sanctions statistics to May 2026, whose own note says the data "will be available as normal for analysis via Stat-Xplore". Both are routes to a Gateshead cut that the claimant count cannot give, particularly on the conditionality regime behind the youth figures above. The accompanying methodology note adds information on failure-to-attend reasons in the sanctions decision statistics, which is worth reading before any local sanctions rate is quoted.

Alongside them, delivery guidance moved. The Jobs Guarantee phase one delivery partner guidance was updated on rebooking and on Multi Agency Public Protection Arrangements, and the adult skills fund devolved grant determination letters for 2026 to 2027 were updated for Strategic Authorities and Foundation Strategic Authorities. Those letters are the skills money that reaches the borough through the strategic authority, and they are the concrete counterpart to the employment support devolution commitment in the Cabinet Statement above.

Sat 22 Aug · NEET thread

A NEET risk-factor analysis surfaces in the DfE collection — three months after publication

Why it matters: Risk factors for becoming NEET: a statistical analysis using linked data was added to the Education and Outcomes Pathways Studies collection this week, but the report itself was published on 19 May 2026 — it is new to us, not new. It uses the second Longitudinal Study of Young People in England linked to longitudinal education outcomes data, at ages 17 to 19 and 20 to 24, and identifies education, health and care plan status, school attendance and attainment, engagement at school, and prior experience of care as risk factors.

What it does and does not do for the live thread. It speaks to the individual-level question — who becomes NEET — which is the half the borough's own work is weakest on, because the local evidence is a tracking count rather than a risk model. It is a national linked-data study, so it will not carry a local authority breakdown, and it therefore cannot close the measurement gap identified here, where the 16 to 17 count is solid and the 18 to 24 picture is not. The value is as a specification: the risk factors it names are all things the council already holds administratively for its own cohort. Worth a full read before 2 September, when the ONS publishes geographical inequalities in NEET young people in England.

Thu 20 & Fri 21 Aug

Exporting business statistics: a new survey and a 2016 to 2024 count

Why it matters: the national survey of registered businesses' exporting behaviours, attitudes and needs 2025 was first published, drawn from fieldwork across 2025, alongside Number of exporting registered businesses in the UK, 2016 to 2024 and refreshed editions of UK trade in numbers and the trade and investment core statistics book. Exporting intensity is one of the few business-level measures with a plausible productivity link that is not already in the borough's dashboard set. The first check on the 2016 to 2024 count is whether its subnational tables reach local authority level or stop at region — if they reach it, that is a new Gateshead series; if they stop at region, it is context. That check has not been done yet and the answer is not assumed here.

Wed 19 Aug

Population projections: the 2022-based subnational projections are under review

Why it matters: the ONS population statistics newsletter confirms that following the mid-2025 population estimates published on 29 July — which revised mid-2022, mid-2023 and mid-2024 to incorporate improved long-term international migration estimates — the ONS "will be undertaking analysis of the 2022-based subnational population projections (SNPPs) against the latest population estimates", plans a projections product by the end of 2026 that reconciles estimates and projections, and will engage users on redeveloping the SNPPs. Long-term international migration next publishes on 26 November 2026.

The read is a caveat, not a finding. Subnational population projections are the denominator under a large share of the borough's per-head measures and the population assumption under the housing requirement in the item above. A projections set that the producer has said is being tested against revised estimates should not be quoted as settled between now and the end of 2026, and anything already published on the 2022-based series should carry that caveat when it is next refreshed.

The high streets consultation still has not opened. The 17 August edition committed to a weekly check on the trailed regulatory package covering the vape shop use class, the revocation of the Gambling Act "aim to permit" duty and adult gaming centre permissions. 22 AugMHCLGHousing Delivery Test measurement rule bookHousing Delivery Test MeaMethod note behind the two measurements.
Nine days that produced more than the fortnight before them. The drought broke: household income, housing delivery and the claimant count all gave the borough new numbers, and two of the three say the same thing in different currencies — Gateshead is holding its position on the things it controls and losing ground on the things it does not. The claimant count is flat while the region rises. Housing delivery crossed back over the line on the strength of one good year. Household income grew 5.8 per cent and the gap to the UK still widened, because the country grew faster. Against that, two failures of intake are worth more than any single item: a government department was renamed in July and this weekly noticed in late August, and England's planning rulebook was replaced on 17 August and reached us on the 22nd. Both were caught by the complete departmental log rather than by a headline, which is the argument for the log. Next edition: Monday 31 August.
